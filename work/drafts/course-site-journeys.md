# Student journeys — the course site for *AI in Business*

*Companion to [`prd-course-site.md`](prd-course-site.md), which specifies the site, and
[`course-site-scorecard.md`](course-site-scorecard.md), which scores the pages against that
specification and reaches 96 %. This document asks the other question. The PRD names one primary
user — "a student in the minor who has just enrolled" — and a single composite user is easy to
satisfy, because a composite has no fears the page happens not to answer. Here that user is split
into four — two Dutch, two international, as the cohort is — each walked through the site stage by
stage, and the site is scored once per persona.*

*Awareness is assumed. These are students who have already enrolled in the minor; the course is
compulsory once they are in it. Nobody arrives at this site by search. The journey starts at the
first click on an LMS link and ends at the interview — the question is never whether they find us,
it is whether the site carries them.*

---

## The five stages

Everything below uses the same spine. It is the student's journey, not the site's structure, so a
stage can be served by any page or by none.

| | Stage | Where the student is | What the site is for, here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **01** | **Orientation** | First visit, from the LMS link, days before the kick-off | Deciding whether this is worth their attention, and what they are in for |
| **02** | **Preparation** | The week before session 1 | Working out what to do before Monday, and doing it |
| **03** | **Commitment** | The four-hour session and the first hand-in | Consulted mid-work: roles, the assignment bar, where the draft goes |
| **04** | **Return** | Weeks 2–6, once a week | One question only — what is due, and what happens next session |
| **05** | **Consolidation** | The assessment period and the interview | What is being judged, on what evidence, and what they say about the course afterwards |

The site's channels are thin on purpose: two published pages (`index.html`, `week-01.html`), the
handbook template as a worked example, and two outbound links (the Commission's Draghi summary,
the minor's own module page). Everything else happens in the room. That is a design choice, and
it is also where three of the four journeys below lose their footing.

## The four personas

The cohort is a mix of Dutch and international students, and the four below are split two and two.
**The course is taught, written and assessed in English** — so the language boundary is not in the
classroom. It falls in two places the site never mentions: the **field visit**, where a regional
manufacturing owner will often be more comfortable in Dutch, and the **desk research**, where much
of what exists on regional SMEs — CBS, the Chamber of Commerce, sector bodies, the regional press —
is Dutch-language.

Internationality is deliberately not one persona's defining problem. It shows up differently in
each: one of the two international students is the strongest technical person in the room, and one
of the two Dutch students has no more feel for regional manufacturing than the exchange student
does. *Dutch* and *local* are not the same axis, and the site's gaps do not respect the binary.

### 1 · Fenna — *"I am going to be the one holding the team back"*

Third-year Business Administration, 21, from the Achterhoek, chose the minor for the
decision-making half. Uses ChatGPT to summarise and rewrite; has never opened a terminal and does
not want to. Read the words *Agent Engineering Lab* in the module list and felt their stomach drop.
Reads carefully, top to bottom, and takes what a page says seriously.

What they have not worked out yet is that they are the team's scarcest asset: they know what the
firms around here make, and they can ring one in Dutch.

**Arrives asking:** can I survive the technical half, and what happens to my team if I can't?
**Leaves if:** the first screen implies the answer is no.
**The risk the site does not see:** relief. When the team discovers who can approach a partner
firm, Fenna is handed a job they are good at — and can trade the technical work away for it,
without anyone naming the trade. That is the exact outcome the no-programming promise exists to
prevent, arriving by a route the site never looks at.

### 2 · Rohan — *"Is there anything here I don't already know?"*

22, third year of a full bachelor in Arnhem, came into the minor from a business-informatics
route. Runs side projects, has shipped a scraper and a Discord bot, uses an agentic CLI most days.
Speaks enough Dutch to order coffee and nothing like enough to interview a factory owner. Skims at
speed, reads for the technical spine, forms a verdict in about ninety seconds.

**Arrives asking:** is the build half real engineering, or six weeks of slides about AI — and is
the research half a tax I pay for the credits?
**Leaves if:** nothing on the page is harder than what they already do. They do not literally leave
— the module is compulsory — they disengage, which is worse, because they then write their team's
PRD alone in twenty minutes.
**The risk the site does not see:** the field visit and the Dutch source base give them a
respectable reason to withdraw into the tool. A course designed so that the research says what
*good* means and the build says how you get there loses its whole point if its strongest builder
has a standing excuse not to research.

### 3 · Joris — *"What will this cost me, and when?"*

23, from Utrecht, works sixteen to twenty hours a week alongside the minor and schedules those
shifts a month out. Goes to the assessment section first, sometimes only. Not cynical —
constrained.

Dutch, and no more able than Rohan to name a regional manufacturer: they have never been to the
Achterhoek and could not say what is made there.

**Arrives asking:** which dates are immovable, what carries the mark, and what happens if I miss
something.
**Leaves if:** the site cannot tell them what the course will demand of them and when. This is the
persona for whom the site's central honesty — *we decide week by week* — is the hardest thing on
the page.

### 4 · Giulia — *"Who is this actually for?"*

24, on Erasmus exchange from Bologna for one semester. Reads and writes English well, has no
Dutch, and is meeting *SME*, *regional manufacturer*, *Draghi* and Dutch team-formation culture in
the same week. Reads on a phone, in short bursts, on the bus.

The best writer of the four, and the one who asks the naive question that exposes what everyone
else assumed. Nothing on the site tells a team that this is worth having.

**Arrives asking:** what is a regional manufacturer, how do I contribute to research about firms I
have never heard of, how do I get into a team when I know nobody — and will I still be here for
the assessment?
**Leaves if:** the course assumes a local knowledge it never supplies — in which case they read
their own gap as a personal deficiency rather than a missing example on the page.

## What the mix does to a team of four

Teams are formed in one twenty-five-minute block in session 1, and stay together for the run. The
site is silent on all four of the following, and each of them lands on the team rather than on the
individual — which is why they do not show up in any single persona's journey.

**1 · The course is in English; the field research is not.** Each team visits one regional SME
partner in weeks 2–4, and that owner may well prefer Dutch. The site does not say what language
the visit runs in, who conducts it, or how a non-Dutch-speaking member takes part. Left unstated,
it resolves itself the obvious way: the Dutch members interview, the international members wait
outside. Two of four students then have no field data of their own in a module assessed partly on
field research.

**2 · The source base is Dutch.** Desk research on regional manufacturing SMEs runs through CBS,
the Chamber of Commerce, sector organisations and the regional press, most of it Dutch-language.
The site never says so — and never points at the obvious response, which is that the platform the
team is building in AEL is precisely the thing that could retrieve, translate and appraise those
sources. That omission turns a language barrier into an exclusion when it could have been the
build's clearest requirement.

**3 · Nobody says who finds the partner firm.** If it is the team's job, the search runs on
personal networks, and the students with a network in the region carry it. If it is the school's
job, saying so removes the problem in one sentence. The site does not say which.

**4 · Teams form themselves, in twenty-five minutes, with no rule.** Self-selection under time
pressure sorts by who people can already talk to easily. The site's *"Do not form a team in
advance — teams are formed in the session"* is the right instinct and does half the job; it stops
pre-formed friend groups and does nothing about sorting in the room. A single stated
composition rule would finish it.

None of these is a fault of the adaptive design. All four are decidable now, and three of them are
one sentence each.

## The scoring system

Same house instrument as the two sibling scorecards — 0–3, self-assessed, a design check rather
than evidence — but pointed at the journey instead of the page.

### Dimensions

Each stage is scored on four dimensions, for each persona:

- **A · Answered** — the question this persona brings to this stage is answered *where they look*,
  not where we would have put it.
- **N · Navigable** — they can get to it, and back, without guessing. Scored in three directions:
  **forward** (the route to the next thing is visible from where they are), **return** (they can
  reach a point they have already been to — the week map, the role table, the assessment section —
  without retracing the whole page), and **lateral** (a question raised on one page can be followed
  to the page that answers it, and followed back).
- **R · Reassured** — the doubt they arrive with is addressed, in terms they would accept.
- **M · Moved** — they know what to do next, and want to do it.

**A and N do not double-count.** If the answer is not on the site at all, that is a failure of
**A**. **N** scores only the route: whether an answer that exists can be reached and left again. A
site can score 3 on A and 0 on N — that is the handbook template, which holds exactly the worked
example the site promises and gives no way back.

### Scale

- **3 — carried.** The site does it, at the point in the journey this persona reaches, and you can
  point at where.
- **2 — carried late or partially.** It is on the site, but downstream of where this persona needed
  it, or true for only some of them.
- **1 — asserted.** The site says it; nothing on the page makes this persona believe it.
- **0 — absent.**

Five stages × four dimensions = **20 items, maximum 60 per persona.**

### The break rule

A journey is sequential, and a page-based score is not. A student who gives up at stage 2 never
experiences stage 4, however good stage 4 is. So:

> **Any stage scoring M ≤ 1 is a break.** Every item in every later stage is capped at 1,
> regardless of what the page would otherwise deserve.

This is the rule that makes the instrument a funnel rather than a checklist. It stops the site
scoring well on its excellent assessment section for a student who stopped reading on the front
page.

**M**, not **N**, triggers the break. A student stopped by bad wayfinding has usually not left —
they have scrolled, guessed, or asked someone. Navigation failure is a tax on every subsequent
stage rather than an exit; it shows up as a low N repeated down the journey, which is what the
per-stage curve is for.

### Report the floor, not the mean

**The cohort score is the lowest of the four persona scores.** Not the average. A site that serves
Rohan at 42 and Joris at 38 is a site that fails a quarter of the room, and the mean hides exactly
the student the course can least afford to lose.

**Target: ≥ 90 % for every persona — 54 / 60 each.** The same bar the sibling scorecards use, held
against the worst-served student rather than the page.

### How to run it

1. Each module owner scores all four personas independently, then the two are compared — the same
   rule the LRD sets for the interview, and the same one `kickoff-scorecard.md` warns is needed
   because the author of the criteria is a poor judge of them.
2. Re-score after each weekly page is published. Stage 04 is the stage the run spends most of its
   time in and the one the site has built least; it should climb as weeks land.
3. **Read down the columns, not only across the rows.** After scoring, check whether the low cells
   cluster — on one stage, one dimension, or one half of the cohort. A gap shared by the two
   international personas is a systematic omission and gets one fix; four separate low scores at
   the same stage for four different reasons need four. The totals will not tell you which you have.
4. **The validity test is not this document.** In session 1, put the site in front of four actual
   students — and make sure at least one of them is an international student — and ask each one
   question: *what could you not find?* Where their answer and this scoring disagree, this scoring
   is wrong.

**Navigation, read down the N column, is the site's most even dimension and its quietest
failure.** It scores 3 where the nav bar happens to name what a persona wants — *Programme* for
Rohan, *Assessment* for Joris — and 1 wherever the journey leaves the two well-built pages: the
handbook template, the unwritten weeks, the person you would ask. Two of those are content that
does not exist yet. One is a bug.

**No journey breaks.** Every stage scores M ≥ 2: the site loses satisfaction, not students. That is
partly the module's compulsory enrolment doing the work, and it should not be read as comfort —
a disengaged Rohan and an unplannable Joris both stay in the room.

**Cohort score: 38 / 60 = 63 %** — a tie at the floor between Joris and Giulia, for unrelated
reasons that share a cause. Against a 90 % target of 54.

**The failures do not cluster on nationality, and that is the finding.** The two Dutch personas
average 39 and the two international personas 40 — indistinguishable. What the totals hide is that
the *reasons* differ: read down the stage-04 column and Fenna and Joris are short of dates and a
contact route, while Rohan and Giulia are short of a language and a network. Score the totals and
the mix looks handled; read the cells and two of the four gaps at the trough belong specifically to
the international half of the room and are named nowhere on the site. The instrument only surfaces
this because it scores per persona and records why — an aggregate would have concealed it.

### The edits, ranked by how much they lift the floor

*All ten were implemented in cycles 2 and 3; kept here as the record of what was done and why.*

### Caveat

The same caveat as every instrument in this folder, and here it bites harder: these four students
do not exist, and they were written by the person who built the site. A persona invented by the
author reliably fails in the ways the author already suspects.

It bites hardest on the half of the cohort the author is not. Fenna and Joris are drawn from a
context the teaching team lives in; Rohan and Giulia are drawn from a guess about one it does not.
The language and network findings above are the ones most likely to be wrong in their particulars —
too pessimistic about how much Dutch a degree-seeking student picks up, too confident that a
regional owner would rather speak Dutch — and they are also the ones no Dutch reader of this
document is well placed to correct. Ask an international student in week 1, not a colleague.

The score that counts is still the one a real student gives when they cannot find something. This
document only decides what to ask them.
