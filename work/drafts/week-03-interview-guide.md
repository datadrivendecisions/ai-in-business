# AIBS — Week 3 assignment: interview guide for the field visit

*Draft for review — not yet session-ready or linked from the site. Minimal prompt, in the style
of the week-1 assignments; to be elaborated once the design is agreed.*

Your team's field visit (ADR-0005) is the point where your research design meets the SME you are
actually building the handbook for. This assignment turns your research proposal and PRD into a
guide for that one conversation — and makes sure the company gets something out of it too, not
just you.

You build the guide in three rounds — your own thinking, then AI, then your partner team — so
that each round has something of the previous one to react to. Keep the guide itself to **1–2
pages**; the round 2 and round 3 logs can run longer, because they are your evidence, not
something you hand the company.

## Round 1 — your own thinking, no AI yet

Sit down as a team and draft this from your research proposal and PRD, before anyone opens an AI
tool. Cover:

1. **What the visit is for, in one paragraph the company can read.** Not your research question —
   *their* stake in it. Why is twenty minutes of their time worth spending on this, for them? This
   paragraph is what you send ahead, or open with.

2. **Questions, traced to your proposal.** For each question, name the sub-question from your
   (reworked) research proposal it answers, and say why this needs *their* answer rather than a
   desk source. A question you could answer by reading a report is a desk-research question, not
   an interview one — cut it. Group questions so the internal-analysis ones (their situation,
   their capabilities, what they've actually tried) are clearly separated from anything that
   checks your external analysis against their experience.

3. **What you give back.** Interviews that only take are the ones companies stop returning calls
   for. Decide, before you go, one concrete thing this SME gets from the conversation — a short
   write-up of what you learn that's useful to them specifically, a quick reflection on where you
   see them on the AI maturity ladder, or an offer to share the finished handbook page once it's
   checked. Say what it is and when they get it. This is what makes the guide "plus": useful to
   both sides, not just a data-collection script with a thank-you at the end.

4. **How you'll run it.** Who asks, who takes notes, how you'll record it (if at all), and the
   consent and confidentiality points you'll cover before a single question — the ones already
   agreed for this module: nobody approaches a company before the consent text is signed off, and
   nothing identifying goes further than agreed. State it here in your own words, as you'll say it
   in the room.

**Do not show this draft to AI before it exists.** The point of round 1 is a guide that is
genuinely yours, with your own blind spots in it — otherwise round 2 has nothing honest to react
to, and round 3 has nothing to catch that AI didn't already smooth over.

## Round 2 — AI makes the questions better

Take the round 1 draft and pressure-test it. A roleplay only improves the guide if it produces
evidence you act on, not a good feeling about your own questions.

1. **Brief the AI as your SME, not as a generic owner.** Use the description from your research
   proposal — sector, size, role, what you already know about their relationship to AI. A vague
   persona produces a vague conversation, and tells you nothing about *this* company.
2. **Run the whole guide in one sitting, in order, without editing between questions.** Fixing a
   question the moment it wobbles tells you nothing about how it would land in the real room.
3. **Read the transcript back for specific tells, not a general impression.** A vague or generic
   answer means the question is too abstract. "I'd have to check" means you're asking for
   something they don't have to hand. "What do you mean by...?" means jargon or ambiguity on your
   side. A one-word answer where you wanted a story means the question is closed when it should be
   open. An answer you could have gotten from a desk source means it's redundant — cut it. A
   guarded or defensive tone means the question reads like an audit, not a conversation, and
   probably needs the "what you give back" piece said earlier, out loud.
4. **Then flip roles.** Ask the same AI, now as an outside reviewer, which of your questions it
   found hardest to answer honestly, and why. Compare that to what actually happened in the
   transcript — the two won't always agree, and where they don't is worth a second look.
5. **Log it as a table:** question → what happened → what you changed → why. That table *is* your
   evidence for "how AI helped" — not a claim that you used it.
6. **Say what this cannot tell you.** An AI playing your SME is not your SME. It won't know their
   actual week, and it won't stay politely vague the way a real person sometimes does. Every
   improvement here is a hypothesis the real interview tests, not a result.

If you have time, run it twice with different persona traits — sceptical of AI versus enthusiastic
about it, for instance. A question that only survives on the friendly version is worth a second
look.

## Round 3 — exchange with your partner team

Send your round 2 guide to the same partner team you have had since week 2 — the peer-gate
pairing, used here for a new document. They read it as if they were about to run this interview
themselves and note:

1. **What would we ask that isn't here?** The gap that two rounds of your own thinking, plus AI,
   still didn't surface.
2. **What confused us, reading it cold?** Anything that needed your team's shared context to make
   sense will need it in the room too.
3. **What would this company probably raise that this guide doesn't ask about?** Their outside
   view of your target SME, not yours.

Revise once more. In your log, mark which peer suggestions changed the guide and which you
deliberately didn't take, and why — the same record your portfolio already asks you to keep for
the module's other gates (ADR-0011): not just that you were questioned, but what you did about it.
