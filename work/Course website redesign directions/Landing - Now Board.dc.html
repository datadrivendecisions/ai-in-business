AI in Business

Six weeks in which your team researches what a regional manufacturer needs to decide about AI — and builds the agentic platform that produces the answer.

You will spend those weeks on one question a real regional manufacturer is asking — should we be doing something with AI, and what? — and answer it properly: researched, published, and produced by a platform your own team builds.

This site holds the purpose, the structure, the assessment and the programme for the two modules that run as one: AIBS and AEL. Week 1 is published; every later week is filled in about one week ahead.

What you make

One deliverable, approached from two sides.

The handbook

Your team writes a handbook about the state of AI for SMEs: what a regional manufacturing owner needs to know and decide. Every week each team publishes one new page — a new page, never an edit to a previous one, so the trail of your thinking stays visible. In the assessment period the pages are merged into one handbook.

Look at the chapter template — the shell every chapter fills, with the quality bar it is measured against.

The platform that produces it

Your team also builds the agentic platform you research and write with: gathering and appraising sources, processing interview material, keeping citations accurate, drafting the page and checking it against the criteria. Your own four researchers are its client.

The handbook never waits on it: for every step it automates you keep a manual route. The platform is judged on whether it works; the page is judged on the research.

How a week runs

1

One session a week, shared by both modules. Both lecturers, the whole cohort. The theme is opened, then you work as a team inside it. Week 1 is a long one — about four hours; later sessions are shaped as we go.

2

Your team publishes one page. A new handbook page on that week's theme, on the shared site.

3

Two gates, both informational. Neither is scored and neither counts towards your mark. They exist to make you revise. They are called gates because your page passes through both before you call it done — not because either can stop you. Nothing here is blocked by a gate.

The Socratic gate — an AI tutor, run by the lecturers, questions your team's thinking. One submission per team per week; questions back, never a verdict and never a score.
The peer gate — a partner team reviews your page against the same published criteria everyone is measured against, and you review theirs.
4

You add a page to your portfolio. Individually, every week, the same four questions. It is the one piece of the week that is yours rather than your team's, and it carries 30 % of both marks.

5

We decide the next week. What we saw in the session and in your pages determines what week N+1 asks for. That week's page appears about a week before the session.

The field visit

Each team visits one regional SME partner, once, somewhere in weeks 2–4. It is a team obligation and the source of the field data your pages rest on.

Finding the partner is not your problem alone. The course maintains a pool of regional firms that have agreed to receive a team, and we match teams to it. Own contact you would rather use? Allowed — tell us first so we can check the consent terms. In week 2 you are told which firm is yours, who to contact there, and what language the visit will run in.

The visit runs in whatever language the partner is comfortable in, and that is often Dutch. Plan for it as a team: an interpreter from inside your own team, a recorded interview your platform transcribes, or a partner happy to work in English. What is not acceptable is a team in which two people conduct the research and two wait outside.

Teams of four, four build roles

Teams are formed in the first session and stay together for the run. Everyone researches in AIBS; in AEL each of you holds one build role, and the role is what you owe the team.

Product manager

Guards the requirements: what the platform is for, what it must let a user do, what is deliberately out of scope. Owns the PRD.

Developer

Drives the agentic CLI: turns requirements into something that runs, and keeps a log of what failed and why.

Tester

Holds the bar: checks output against the quality criteria before anyone else sees it, and says when it does not clear them.

Deployer

Gets the work published and reproducible: the page reaches the shared site, and someone else can run what you ran.

How to choose. Take the role you want to get better at, not the one you are already good at — none of the four is graded on how well you arrived at it. If you have never opened a terminal, developer is a legitimate choice and the tool is built on the assumption that you will make it. None of the four roles is a spectator.

The fifth job, which is not a role. Your team also has to reach a real firm, interview the people in it, and read sources about a region — and that work is not evenly easy for four different people. Whoever can approach a local company, or read a Dutch source, is holding something the team needs. Share it out: do not hand it to one person, and do not let it stop anyone building. Teams that drift into the ones who talk to the firm and the ones who touch the tool produce the weakest handbooks, every time.

When you form a team, aim for four people who do not already work together, and for a mix: at least one comfortable at a command line, at least one who knows this region or can work in Dutch. If your team has neither, say so in the session — we would rather fix that in week 1 than in week 4.

How you are assessed

70 %
One joint criterion-based interview

Your whole team and both lecturers, in English, in the assessment period. Each lecturer questions from their own side and marks their own module.

30 %
Your individual portfolio

Built weekly, submitted once. Both lecturers read the same portfolio from their own side: there is one portfolio, not two.

Everything else — the team's weekly pages, the two gates, the drafts — stays formative. It is the raw material your portfolio is written from, not the grade. Your two marks are individual, and the portfolio is the reason they can be: four people share a team page; only you can say what you did to it.

Two independent marks, one per module. A strong technical answer is not marked up on research grounds, or the reverse.

The evidence

Your portfolio, your team's frozen submissions — the pages as they stood before the merge — and your team's gate and iteration history: what the tutor asked, what your peers said, what you changed.

What is not evidence

The gates themselves. Neither changes your mark and neither is a verdict on your page; both are prompts to revise. What you did with the questions goes in your portfolio, and that is a different thing.

Deliverables at the end

The consolidated handbook, your team's frozen contribution to it, your team's platform with the documents that specify it — starting with the week 1 PRD — and your own portfolio.

The portfolio

One document per student, yours alone, added to every week. Short by design — about a page a week — and it always answers the same four questions:

1

What did the team publish this week? The page, in a line or two.

2

What did you contribute to it? Specifically, and without inflating it. "I wrote the section on vendor lock-in and found three of the six sources" is worth more than "I helped with the research".

3

What did the two gates ask, and what did you change? Not whether you agreed — what moved. A question you rejected, with a reason, counts as much as one you acted on.

4

What did your build role produce? The PRD revision, the failure log, the test results, the deployment.

Write it the same week, not at the end. A portfolio assembled from memory in the assessment period is visible as one, and it is worth less than a thin record kept as you went. An honest small contribution scores better than an unsupportable large one — the portfolio makes a claim; the interview is where the claim is tested.

The two criterion sets — one per module — are written by each module's owner and published here before they are used to judge anything. Until they are on this site, they do not exist.

Why this course exists

In September 2024 Mario Draghi handed the European Commission a report on why Europe is falling behind the United States and China. Its central finding is not that Europe lacks ideas. It is that Europe is slow to diffuse technology into the businesses it already has — and the place diffusion stalls hardest is the small and mid-sized firm. Fragmented markets, regulatory complexity, no capital to spare, no in-house skills, no scale. The gap in productivity is, to a large extent, a gap in adoption.

Most of those firms are within an hour's drive of this building. They are being told, loudly and from every direction, that they must "do something with AI". Very few have anyone who can tell them what, or whether, or first. This course puts you in that gap as a researcher who can find out and explain, not as a consultant.

The Commission's one-page summary is your pre-reading for week 1.

What you will be able to do afterwards

Research

Turn a firm's vague worry into an answerable question

You take "should we do something with AI?" from a real regional manufacturer, turn it into a research question with sub-questions, answer it with desk and field research, and write the answer for someone who will act on it.

Judgement

Tell whether an AI output is any good

Most people arrive thinking the skill is producing text with a model. The scarce one is verification. You practise it every week: you generate, then you check the claim, the source and the reasoning before you put your name on it.

Build

Direct an agentic system to do real work

You specify, build, test and deploy a working platform by instructing an agentic CLI rather than hand-writing code — and you learn what that mode of working is good at, and where it fails without telling you.

Publish

Ship something a stranger reads

Your work is published, cited and read outside the classroom. It is a chapter of a handbook the next cohort builds on.

Where it sits, and what it assumes

AI in Business is the AI strand of HAN's minor Data Driven Decision Making in Business. It assumes what the minor already gives you: that you can frame a business problem, work with data and evidence, and defend a recommendation. It does not assume that you can program. In the technical track you direct an agentic command-line tool; the design rule is that a non-coder must be able to run it, and that every automated step keeps a manual fallback.

The course is taught, written and assessed in English. It does not assume you are Dutch, or that you already know this region and its firms — one of the things week 1 does is make sure no team is short of that knowledge. Where the work runs into Dutch, the team solves that together.

Self-assessment · week 1

The AI Worker Maturity Scale

Sixteen questions that place you on a six-level scale from Bystander to Compounding, then name the single thing standing between you and the next level. Unlike most maturity models it measures a person, not a company, and it asks for observable facts about how you work rather than how capable you feel.

Use it before or during the first session. The level it gives you — plus the gate it says is holding you — is a good opening for your first portfolio entry.

It is not a validated instrument and says so on its own front page: it is composed from published research rather than tested as a scale. Treat the result as a conversation with yourself, not a score.

The maturity scale is reproduced here from the AI Wiki, where it is maintained and where its calibration record and sources live. It keeps its own look on purpose — a tool that happens to live on the course site, not a page of the course.
