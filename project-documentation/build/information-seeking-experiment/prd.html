1 · Problem and users

Week 3 runs an experiment on the students themselves: does an AI devil’s advocate reduce biased information seeking — the habit of reading the things that agree with you? The design is within-person and counterbalanced, so every student does the task twice, once with the agent and once without.

Run on paper, the session collapses. Someone has to shuffle sixteen cards differently for each of thirty-two students, enforce a budget of eight, time each choice, collect two rounds of answers, and get thirty-two results into one place before the room loses interest. None of that is interesting work and all of it is error-prone.

Three users, wanting different things.

  • The student wants to get through it without being confused about what they are allowed to do, and to leave understanding what happened to them.
  • The lecturer wants thirty-two results in one place, a paired comparison on screen, and no laptop admin during a 240-minute block.
  • The module owners want data that survives being questioned: randomisation recorded, a fixed codebook, nothing invented after the fact.

A fourth party matters and is not a user: the student as participant. They consent, they may withdraw, and nothing they type may leave their own browser.

2.3 Round 1, step 2 — the agent, and why it comes here

The order is the experiment. What is measured is which sources a student chooses to open, so the agent has to arrive while that choice is still ahead of them. An agent appearing after the menu could not move the measurement at all — both rounds would produce the same number by construction.

The agent itself is modelled on the randomised trial in Chiang et al. 2024, where 350 participants in 120 groups met an LLM devil’s advocate under five conditions. Two of its findings shape this design: the variant that objected to the recommendation rather than to the group’s majority produced the higher decision accuracy, so ours objects to the position and never to the person; and the interactive variant beat the one that spoke once, which we cannot have, since a page that makes no network request cannot hold a conversation. What we keep is the target; what we give up is the interactivity, and it is the stronger half we are giving up. The case for using a machine at all rather than a colleague is in Csaszar et al. 2024 §2.3: a human devil’s advocate costs days of skilled work and carries social inhibition about contradicting a senior person, and a model carries neither — an argument first made about human advocates in Schwenk 1984.

It follows that the prompt carries the claim and the student’s own locked sentence, and no sources, because none have been read yet.

The prompt describes the position in the third person and asks for questions in the second. That is deliberate. Models preserve the asker’s face 45 percentage points more often than humans do on advice queries, and affirm both sides of a conflicting position in 48% of cases depending on who is asking (Cheng et al. 2025) — so a prompt saying “ask me about my position” hands the model a face to protect. “A student wrote this” gives it less of one, while asking for questions addressed to you keeps them usable by the person reading them.

2.4 Round 1, step 3 — the source menu, the screen that is the experiment

Sixteen cards, one at a time, in an order shuffled for this student alone. Eight opens. No going back. The position stays on screen, because the measurement is about this position.

One at a time is a choice, not a limitation of the screen. Information presented sequentially produces a stronger preference for supporting material than the same information presented all at once, because each step focuses attention back on the decision already made (Jonas et al. 2001). Real searching is sequential, so the realistic condition is also the one where the effect is largest. The effect being looked for is itself well established: a meta-analysis of selective-exposure studies put the preference for congenial over uncongenial information at d = 0.36 (Hart et al. 2009).

2.5 Round 1, step 4 — your position again

The same two controls as step 1, with the original sentence shown but not editable. What is recorded is whether it moved.

2.6 Round 1, step 5 — closing questions, asked per round

Per round, not once at the end. The comparison that makes this session memorable is between how well a round went and how it felt, and a single set of answers at the finish cannot be attributed to either condition. Six workload items and two perception items, each time.

The comparison is worth building for because it has been found three times over four decades. Groups with the interactive devil’s advocate had the highest decision accuracy and the lowest self-perceived performance and perceived teamwork quality (Chiang et al. 2024). Dialectical inquiry and devil’s advocacy beat consensus on decision quality while their participants reported less satisfaction and less acceptance of the outcome (Schweiger et al. 1986). And an LLM devil’s advocate raised satisfaction but through presence rather than content, with cognitive load edging up (Lee et al. 2025). If the room reproduces any of that on its own data, nobody will need convincing.

2.7 Round 2 — what it is for, and what changes

Round 2 is not a repeat. It is the other half of the measurement. Each student is their own control, and the comparison that matters is between a student’s two rounds rather than between one student and another. Without a second round there is no experiment at all — only a description of how thirty-two people read once.

That design choice is what makes eight teams workable. Comparing students against each other would mean absorbing every difference in reading habit, confidence and prior knowledge as noise. Comparing a student against themselves removes all of it.

What changes

 Round 1Round 2
ClaimClaim 1Claim 2 — never claim 1 again
CardsDeck 1, its own 16Deck 2, its own 16, crossed 4/4/4/4 the same way
OrderShuffled for this studentShuffled again, independently
Agent (teams 1–4)Present — 5 stepsAbsent — 4 steps
Agent (teams 5–8)Absent — 4 stepsPresent — 5 steps

The claim has to change, and that is not a preference. A student who has just read eight sources on claim 1 and committed to a position on it would, on meeting it again, be searching their memory rather than searching the menu. The second round would measure recall and tell us nothing about information seeking.

What must not change

Everything the measurement rests on is held identical, or the two rounds are not comparable and the paired difference means nothing:

  • The budget of eight opens, and the rule that a passed card does not come back.
  • The position locked before the first card, in the same words on screen.
  • The same closing questions, in the same order.
  • The wording of every instruction, down to the sentence about what opening costs.

What the student is told between the rounds

As little as possible, and nothing about the agent. A student who works out that the assistant is the thing being tested will behave differently in whichever round it appears — which is the one threat to this design that no amount of counterbalancing repairs.

What it delivers

A second round block in the result line, carrying its own counts, its own timing, its own movement flag and its own subjective scores:

|r2:none:c2:op8:cong6:sopp1:s142:moved0:tlx38:q9

Two blocks make one paired observation. The difference is always taken as agent round minus no-agent round, which means it is subtracted in opposite directions for the two halves of the room:

 Agent roundPaired difference for that student
Teams 1–4Round 1r1 − r2
Teams 5–8Round 2r2 − r1

A negative number means the student read less one-sidedly in the round where the agent spoke. Averaging the two halves this way also cancels two things at once: the effect of claim 2 being a different question from claim 1, and the effect of simply being further into the session. Both fall out of the arithmetic because each is added to one half and subtracted from the other.

One thing it does not cancel. A student who met the devil’s advocate in round 1 carries it into round 2 — they may read more even-handedly the second time because of the first. Counterbalancing removes the average effect of that carryover but not its interaction with the agent, and nothing in the design can. It belongs in the write-up as a stated limitation rather than a solved problem.

2.8 The end — submitting, which is the consent act

Two round blocks, each carrying its own counts, its own timing and its own subjective scores, go to the class service. Pressing the button is how a student takes part, and not pressing it is how they decline — the same choice closing the tab used to be, made explicit.

What travels is numbers. The position sentences and the assistant’s reply never leave the laptop, which is what keeps the payload uninteresting to read and keeps NFR-11 a property of the wiring rather than a promise.

2.9 The instructor dashboard

Owners only, and live: submissions appear while the room is still working, which is the reason the service exists at all. Thirty-two people watching a difference take shape and then being asked what it licenses is the session.

The interpretation is selected, not written. Which of a fixed set of sentences appears is decided by where the interval sits relative to zero and to the smallest effect this sample could have detected — and it cannot render unless both of those numbers are on screen beside it. A model composing the sentence would produce something fluent and overconfident about an underpowered study, which is the failure this whole session exists to teach against.

3 · Output qualities — what good data looks like

3a. Invariants — every student, every round, pass/fail

  • The card order shown is recorded, not just the choices. Without it, position effects and stance effects cannot be told apart.
  • The position sentence is locked before the agent speaks and before the first card appears, and cannot be edited afterwards.
  • Workload and perception are recorded once per round, so each set of answers belongs to a known condition.
  • Exactly eight opens, or fewer if the student runs out of cards; never nine.
  • The result line identifies a self-chosen code and a team, and carries no name.
  • The counts reconcile: opened = congenial + uncongenial.

3b. Qualities — judged on the class as a whole

  • Comparability. Every student met the same sixteen cards. Any difference between them is about them, not about what they were handed.
  • Reconstructability. From the thirty-two lines alone, a second person can recompute every number the lecturer screen displayed.
  • Legibility under doubt. A student who asks “how do you know I was biased?” can be shown their own line and the codebook, and follow the arithmetic.

4 · Known constraints

  • One origin, two routes, and nothing else. CLAUDE.md allows JavaScript on a tool-*.html page only if it is self-contained. ADR-0016 gives that up for this one named page and no other, so the exception cannot spread by analogy. It still rules out hosting the model: the agent runs in the student’s own assistant.
  • localStorage is still where the session lives. The service receives a submission at the end; it is not the working store. That is what lets the tool finish a session with the service down, and the manual paste remains as the fallback path rather than as the primary one.
  • The site is public. The sixteen cards are in the page source, so the stance and quality labels cannot be, or the experiment is readable before it is run.
  • One 240-minute block, shared with the rest of week 3. The tool gets perhaps 150 minutes.
  • Light only, brand system, sticky nav and footer, copied from index.html like every other tool.
  • --text-muted on --bg-cream is 4.26:1 and fails AA. Not that pair, anywhere.

5 · Out of scope (for now)

  • Calling a model. The agent runs in the student’s own assistant, from a prompt the tool supplies.
  • Writing the conclusion. The tool displays numbers and asks about them. Stating a finding would put the verdict-giving behaviour the experiment is testing inside the instrument that measures it — and would take from students the one thing the session exists to give them. There is evidence for the narrow claim too: across six studies the presence of a conclusive recommendation consistently amplified confirmation bias while its absence mitigated it, and the source of the information mattered far less than its structure (Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans, 2026). A tool that graded rather than asked would also be unreliable at it — human–LLM agreement on student essays scored against a four-criterion rubric was non-significant, worst on exactly the interpretive criteria a project rubric uses (Gaggioli et al. 2025) — and the order of presentation alone can flip an LLM judge’s verdict (Wang et al. 2024).
  • Scoring anyone. ADR-0011 keeps the week’s gates informational and unscored; this is not a back door.
  • Cross-device or cross-session sync, and any account for a student. The service knows a self-chosen code and nothing that identifies anyone.
  • A general-purpose survey builder. Two claims, sixteen cards each, hard-coded.
  • Keeping the data. A scheduled purge deletes it at the end of the teaching day — a job, not an intention.
  • A model writing the interpretation. The dashboard selects the sentence the interval permits. It does not compose one.

6 · Open questions and assumptions

  • Where do the stance and quality labels live? They cannot sit in the published page. Either the lecturer view holds the codebook and it is pasted in at analysis time, or the labels stay out of site/ entirely and are applied to the lines afterwards in a spreadsheet. Owner: the module owners, before the menu is built.
  • What happens if a student passes over more than eight cards? They reach the end with budget unspent. Assumed acceptable, since the index is a proportion of what was opened — but worth confirming that a student with three opens is not silently compared with one who opened eight.
  • Moving the agent forward costs a measure. With the agent before the menu, it can no longer be observed changing a student’s final conclusion. That was a secondary outcome and the trade was made deliberately; if the owners want it back, the agent would have to appear twice, which lengthens the session and confounds the two touches.
  • Two claims are needed and neither is chosen, and this is where most of the risk in the build sits. What they have to satisfy is now written down as the CL rules in the blueprint, and how to find them — draft in comparison form, pilot for the split, start with the hardest cell — is phase 0 of the build plan. The single criterion most easily missed: a claim of the form “X before Y” has commercial advocates on both sides, where “adopt X” has them on only one, and the weak-opposing cell then cannot be filled. Four candidates drawn from the AI Wiki are written up in work/drafts/week-03-candidate-claims.md. Owner: the AIBS owner.
  • Is the extract enough, or must they open the original? Assumed the extract is enough for the clock; the link is there for anyone who wants it. If reading the original matters, the session does not fit.
