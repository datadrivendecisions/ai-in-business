Phase 0 — The deck and the codebook

Goal. Two claims survive the CL rules, and thirty-two real sources exist, each with a stance and a quality settled by two people who agreed.

Most of the risk in this whole build is here. A claim that fails cannot be rescued by better cards, and the failure usually shows up as difficulty finding the fourth source in a cell. When that happens the difficulty is the evidence telling you the claim is lopsided — discard the claim rather than lowering the bar for the card.

The pilot is the first session. Decided by the owners on 18 September 2026. There is no separate hands-up pilot before the session: the sides students lock before reading are the pilot’s split, the seconds until they choose one are its timing, and the pilot audit in the lecturer view (AN-8) reports every criterion the data can settle. Phase 0 closes on what that audit says after the session. The cost is that a claim which fails is found out afterwards rather than before: it is replaced for the next run, and this run’s comparison is reported with the miss written beside it.

What the tester does. Opens a plain listing of all 32 cards, counts four in every cell, follows three links at random and finds the quoted extract actually in the document.

Spec. CL-1–CL-10, SM-5, SM-6, SM-7, SM-10, SM-11, MS-7, AN-8

Subtasks

The comparison form is what makes CL-3 satisfiable: almost nobody publishes marketing material arguing against buying software, so a proposition has an easy weak-supporting cell and a near-impossible weak-opposing one, while X and Y each have their own vendors selling against the other.

Phase 4 — Closing questions and timing

Goal. The subjective measures and the clock are captured per round, so the “better but feels worse” comparison is possible at all. Asked once at the finish they would belong to neither condition and the comparison would be impossible.

What the tester does. Completes a whole session and gets a result line carrying a workload total and both timings.

Spec. MS-5, MS-6

Subtasks

  • ☑ 4.1 Six workload items and two perception items, 7-point — shown at the end of each round, not once at the finish.
  • ☑ 4.2 Total the workload to 0–100.
  • ☑ 4.3 Time each card and each menu; add seconds per round to the line.
  • ☑ 4.4 Extend the line format so each round block carries its own timing, movement flag and scores. Bump the version token if the shape changes.
  • Where this stands. Built and machine-checked. The six workload items are the NASA-TLX dimensions on a 7-point scale; performance is asked the right way up and totalled upside down, since on this instrument doing well is a low cost. Physical demand is near the floor for a reading task and is kept anyway, because an instrument is only comparable with itself if it stays whole. The two perception items are satisfaction and confidence, which is the pairing the “better but feels worse” literature in the PRD turns on. Timing is wall clock from a card appearing to the move to the next one, so an opened card's time includes reading the extract; a reload restarts the current card's clock rather than counting the minutes a laptop was shut. What is left is a tester completing a session unaided inside 25 minutes, which is the one thing none of this can tell you.

Test gate

  • ☑ 8 items present, 7 points each, asked 2 times per session (MS-6, inspection)
  • ☑ Card timings within 100 ms of wall clock over 5 cards (MS-5, test)
  • ☑ A complete line parses under the phase 5 parser once it exists
  • ☐ Tester completes one session unaided and in under 25 minutes

Phase 5 — The lecturer view

Goal. Thirty-two lines become one paired comparison, and a question.

What the tester does. Opens the page with ?instructor, pastes 32 fixture lines plus 2 broken ones, sees 32 accepted and 2 listed as rejected, reads the difference and the interval, and finds a question on screen where a conclusion would be.

Since ADR-0016 this is the fallback path, not the primary one — what happens when the service is down. It is still built first, because it is the arithmetic the dashboard also needs and it can be tested without any infrastructure at all.

Spec. AN-1, AN-2, AN-3, AN-4, AN-5, AN-6, AN-7

Subtasks

  • ☑ 5.1 Copy the instructor-view block from site/index.html verbatim; mark the lecturer section data-audience="instructor". Add nothing to the block.
  • ☑ 5.2 Paste box; parse lines; report accepted and rejected, listing every rejection.
  • ☑ 5.3 Take the codebook as pasted input and apply it to the card ids.
  • ☑ 5.4 Compute the two means, the paired difference and a 95% interval.
  • ☑ 5.5 Draw the two bars.
  • ☑ 5.6 Show the smallest detectable effect for the n entered.
  • ☑ 5.7 Put a question and a writing box where a conclusion would go.
  • ☑ 5.8 A kappa box: four cell counts in, kappa out, with observed and chance agreement shown beside it. Used by students in the debrief, not by the analysis.
  • ☑ 5.9 Five fixture datasets with values from a reference implementation.
  • Where this stands. Built and machine-checked. The codebook parser is deliberately tolerant: it reads work/drafts/week-03-codebook.md as it stands, ignoring every line and every part of a line that is not a card id followed by a cell code, because asking an owner to reformat the answer key before a session is asking for a transcription error in the one input nothing downstream can check. A 15.8 KB paste of that file yielded 32 cards in a browser. The interval uses a t quantile computed from the incomplete beta rather than a table, so it is right for whatever n turns up; the detectable effect is solved from the non-central t rather than approximated, because the usual closed form is out by .04 at half-group sizes and wrong in the reassuring direction. Both were checked against scipy, and the power figure against a 200,000-run simulation. What is left is the one check that cannot be automated by the person who wrote the screen: AN-3, that nothing on it states a finding.

Test gate

  • ☑ 32 good lines → 32 accepted (AN-1, test)
  • ☑ 2 malformed lines → 2 listed, 0 silent drops (AN-6, test)
  • ☑ Difference and interval match the reference on 5 datasets to 3 dp (AN-2, test)
  • ☐ 0 concluding sentences on screen, ≥ 1 question (AN-3, inspection by someone who did not write it)
  • ☑ Detectable effect within 0.02 of a reference value (AN-4, analysis)
  • ☑ Kappa matches hand computation on 4 matrices (AN-5, test), each shown with its 2×2 and chance figure (AN-7, inspection)
  • ☑ Without ?instructor, the lecturer section is not visible

Phase 7 — The service and the submit button

Goal. A student’s numbers reach a service they did not have to type into, and the session survives that service being down.

What the tester does. Finishes a session, presses Submit my numbers, and watches the stored count go up by one. Then turns off the network, finishes a second session, and finds the copy button and the line exactly where they were. Then asks an owner to delete their row by code, and watches the count go back down.

Spec. SV-1–SV-6, SV-11, AG-1

Subtasks

  • ☑ 7.1 A Cloud Run service in the course project, EU region, separate deployment from the gate (SV-11). — Deployed 18 September 2026 by an owner, with deploy.sh: experiment-service in europe-west4, on a Firestore database of its own (experiment) that its account alone may use, so it cannot read the gate’s owner_reports/. The first live test found the CORS preflight answered 502: a 204 carried a body, which the local server allowed and Cloud Run’s front end does not. Fixed in app.py, with a check; it needs one more deploy.
  • ☑ 7.2 POST /submit: accepts one submission, rejects any payload containing a string field that is not the code or the team (SV-1).
  • ☑ 7.3 Firestore submissions/, keyed by session and code. Last write wins for a repeated code.
  • ☑ 7.4 An owner-only delete route, by code (SV-5).
  • ☑ 7.5 A scheduled purge that empties the collection at the end of the teaching day (SV-4).
  • ☑ 7.6 In the tool: the submit screen, its plain statement of what is sent and what is not, and the button (SV-2).
  • ☑ 7.7 In the tool: on any failure, fall back to the line and the copy button without blocking the finish (SV-6).
  • ☑ 7.8 Pin the allowed origin. The tool calls one host and two routes; nothing else (AG-1).

Where this stands. Built and machine-checked, and unplugged from anything. The service is experiment_service/, which is not in site/ and is therefore not published. check-service.py beside this plan drives every route end to end through WSGI — no port, no container, no cloud project — with the clock injected, which is what lets the retention check fast-forward a day instead of waiting one. 25 checks, and each of the five that matter was sabotaged and confirmed to fail.
SV-1 is held the strong way round. The service does not strip prose out of what arrives; it refuses a payload carrying any field schema.py does not name, so the day someone adds a student’s sentence to the tool’s payload the submission is rejected rather than stored. 18 payloads carrying something they should not, 18 refused.
Two things are narrower than the record asked for, both free. The dashboard never returns a code — each row carries a salted hash instead, so two submissions from one person are still visibly one person while nobody reading the response learns whose. And retention is enforced three times: the scheduled purge, a Firestore TTL policy, and every read dropping an expired row before returning it. A retention rule resting on one cron job firing is an intention, and ADR-0016 was explicit that this is not one.
One bug worth recording, because it was mine and it was easy. The first draft of /purge accepted X-CloudScheduler: true as proof of who was calling. That is a header, not a credential, and any caller can send it — the draft let a student wipe the room’s data mid-session with one curl. The scheduler now carries its own bearer token, kept apart from the owners’, and the check that catches the spoof is in check-service.py.
AG-1 changed meaning under ADR-0016 and its check had to change with it. check-session.mjs used to assert that the file contained no network call at all, which passed for four phases and was the wrong assertion: a test that something is absent reads later as a requirement that it stay absent. It now enumerates every way out of the page, accounts for each, and forbids the transports this page has no business having.
SERVICE.origin ships empty, and that is not a placeholder waiting to be tidied. With it empty the page makes no request at all, the consent screen says so in its own words, and the finish is the one phase 5 shipped. Putting a URL there is the single edit that turns submission on, which is why the service can be deployed before a session without changing what a student meets until an owner decides.

Phase 8 — The dashboard and its interpretation

Goal. The owners see the comparison take shape while the room is still working, with a sentence that says what the interval permits and never more.

What the tester does. Opens the dashboard with owner credentials and sees nothing without them. Submits from another machine and watches the count and the bars move within ten seconds. The codebook moves to Secret Manager and arrives with the rows, to the owners only; the credential rides in the link after the #, which never leaves the browser, and the page takes it out of the address bar before the first request and keeps it for the tab alone. Needs one more deploy to put the codebook secret in place.

Where this stands. Built, machine-checked, and driven in a real browser against a running service.
“Matches phase 5’s arithmetic” is true by construction rather than by agreement. The dashboard does not compute anything: it turns each stored row into the record shape the paste box produces and hands it to the same pairUp and summarise that phase 5 checked against scipy. There is one implementation, and the check runs 24 pairs down both paths and compares all 23 figures to 3 dp. A second implementation that happened to agree would be a worse answer to AN-2 than this.
One template could never have appeared. The first draft had four regions, the fourth for an interval narrower than the smallest difference the sample could detect. It is unreachable at every sample size: a 95% interval runs about 1.4 times the width of an 80%-power detectable effect — 1.52 at n = 3, 1.41 at 32, 1.40 at 2000 — because both are fixed multiples of the same standard error. The fixture built to reach it landed in the spanning region instead, which is how it was caught. The set is three, the fact is now said inside the spanning sentence where it belongs, and a check sweeps sixteen sample sizes to insist every stored sentence is reachable by some real summary. A template nobody can select reads like a case the page handles.
SV-10 is structural. A template is a function over the numbers it needs, and governingNumbers() returns null if any of them is missing — so a sentence has nothing to render into and the panel is absent rather than hedged. Seven crippled summaries, 0 sentences drawn.
SV-9’s other half is AN-3, and they pull against each other: the dashboard must say something, and it must state no finding. What each sentence states is what an interval of that width over a sample of that size does and does not license, which is a fact about the instrument and not about the world. The check runs an 11-phrase verdict list over the set and insists every sentence names something the data cannot do.

What can run in parallel

Derived from which files a phase writes to, because merge conflicts are a file-level matter and have nothing to do with how independent two phases feel.

Phase 0 is the only thing that can run beside anything else, because it is the only phase that does not touch the tool. That is convenient, since it is also the slowest and the one that needs two people: one person hunts sources while the other builds phases 1–2.

Phases 1–6 share one file and must run in order. Two people editing the same tool page concurrently will spend longer merging than they saved.

Phase 7 splits, and the split is useful. Its server half touches no file in this repository, so the service can be built alongside phases 1–4 by someone else entirely. Its client half — the submit screen and the fallback — is back in the tool page and queues behind them. Settle the submission payload before the two halves diverge; it is the second contract in this build, after the result line, and the same discipline applies.

One interface has to be fixed before phases 2 and 5 can be worked on by different people: the result line format. It is the contract between the student’s session and the lecturer’s analysis. Settle its shape in phase 2 and do not change it afterwards without bumping the version token.
