Theme 3 — Can this shop trust AI with its data, its output, and its people?

This chapter helps an owner decide which of three trust questions they actually have to answer this year, and which one they can leave.

Team 7 · cohort 2026 · version 1.0 · SME: persona — a 30-person precision metalworking shop in the Achterhoek, family-owned, one office manager who handles all IT alongside the invoicing · sources last appraised: 6 September 2026

What last week's gate asked, and what we did.

On calling the transparency obligation "a house rule". We were wrong, and the correction changed how this chapter is organised. The obligation is Article 50, it is law rather than practice, and it came into force on 2 August 2026 — last month. What we meant, and said badly, is that for this shop the obligation to mark machine-generated content mostly falls on the provider of the tool, while what the shop itself needs is a rule about its own staff. Those are two different things and we had collapsed them into one. Section "Where the obligation actually sits" below is what replaced that sentence.

On the specialist quotation tools we could not appraise. We went back and looked properly. There is no independent evaluation of these products that we can find: the trade press reprints vendor material and the vendors publish case studies about themselves. Rather than keep saying we cannot appraise them, we worked out what an owner can do without an evaluation, and that is the "How to buy something nobody has reviewed" section.

On switching cost mattering more than vendor nationality. We still think this is right and it holds up in the reading we have done since.

The question, for this kind of SME

"Can we trust AI" is three questions wearing one coat, and they have different answers and different deadlines for a firm of this size. Can we trust it with our data — where does what we put in end up. Can we trust what comes out — and how would we know if we could not. And what does it do to the people who do the work. An owner who tries to settle all three at once settles none.

The data: a contract question before it is a technology question

For this shop the sensitive material is the customer's drawing, and where it may be processed is set by the shop's contract with that customer. That is not a preference the owner can weigh against convenience; it is an obligation they have already signed. The emails around a drawing carry names and contact details and are personal data under the GDPR whatever the drawing is, which brings in a different set of rules again.

Microsoft's own documentation sets out where its enterprise services store and process data, and for a firm inside the EU Data Boundary the answer is: within it, with stated exceptions the documentation lists. We use the vendor for that because a vendor is the right source for where its own servers are. Whether that arrangement satisfies a particular customer contract is a question the vendor cannot answer and the customer can.

Where the obligation actually sits

Article 50 of the AI Act sets transparency obligations, including that providers of systems generating synthetic text must mark their output as artificially generated in a machine-readable form (AI Act, Article 50). It entered into application on 2 August 2026. For this shop the practical reading is that the obligation to mark output sits mostly with whoever supplies the tool, not with a thirty-person metalworking firm using it.

What the firm still needs is separate and smaller: staff should know when they are reading a machine-generated draft, because a quotation that goes out with a signature on it is a liability whoever drafted it. That is an internal rule the owner writes. Last week we called both of these things "a house rule" and that was wrong: one is law and one is management, and an owner who confuses them will either over-comply or assume the whole Act is somebody else's problem.

We are reading legislation, not practising law, and Article 50 is new enough that how it is enforced for small deployers is not yet visible.

The output: trust is a measurement, not a feeling

The question "can we rely on what it produces" has no general answer and a very specific one. For the quotation use case from last week's chapter, reliability means: of the drafts it produces, how many go out unchanged. That number is knowable in a month and unknowable by reading vendor material.

Stanford's AI Index is useful for the direction of travel on model capability and is not about firms like this one, so we use it for orientation rather than for any number an owner would act on. The measurement that matters here is the shop's own, on its own quotations.

How to buy something nobody has reviewed

There is no independent evaluation of sector-specific quotation tools that we could find. Trade coverage reprints vendor announcements, and the case studies are published by the vendors. That is a real gap and an owner still has to decide, so:

  1. Ask the vendor for two customers of your size in your sector, and call them without the vendor present.
  2. Ask what the exit looks like: can you get your data out, in what format, and what does it cost.
  3. Run it on last year's quotations before you run it on this year's, so you can compare against work you already know the answer to.
  4. Buy the shortest term you can, even at a worse rate, until you have your own number from step 3.

The people: named here, researched in week 5

The third trust question is what this does to the office manager whose week it changes. We are not answering it in this chapter, because our field visit is this week and we would rather ask them than guess. What we will say now is that every source we have read treats the person operating the tool as a detail, and that is a gap in the literature and not only in our chapter.

First five steps for an owner

  1. Pull the customer contract that governs your most sensitive drawings and read the processing clause. Everything else waits on it.
  2. Decide which of the three trust questions you actually have to answer this year. It is probably the data one.
  3. Write the staff rule about machine-generated drafts on one side of A4, and separate it from anything you owe under the AI Act.
  4. Pick one output and define what "reliable" would mean as a number you could count in a month.
  5. Count it before you buy anything, so you have a baseline that is yours.

What this chapter does not cover

The people question, deliberately, until we have talked to someone. And we have not tested any of the buying advice above against an owner who has actually bought one of these tools — it is reasoning from what we could not find, which is weaker than evidence.
