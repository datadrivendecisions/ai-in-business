Part 1 — Context & positioning

1.1 What this LRD is, and isn't

This document does not re-argue the mission — the EU competitiveness case, Draghi, the Competitiveness Compass — which is the starting point of the AIBS owner's proposal (§1.4) and the subject of the week-2 research assignment (Part 8), not a claim this LRD has to defend. It does not describe the handbook beyond what §1.3 needs: the handbook's published form is the template every cohort fills, and the programme design that page used to carry is superseded by this document. It does not re-derive the three technical sketches — they live in module-redesign-sketches.md — and it does not restate the AIBS owner's proposal in full; §1.4 carries what this LRD takes from it. What this LRD adds:

  • A formal Learner Requirement Document for the two modules together, in the format HAN already uses for workshop and application design (see the LRD-Workshop precedents in background-docs/) — learning outcomes, design criteria, functional/non-functional requirements, deliverables, acceptance criteria.
  • A committed technical level for AEL: Sketch 3, the most ambitious of the three. What AEL builds at that level is no longer a client prototype: under ADR-0006 it is the research platform the AIBS track works with (§6.1), and the handbook must not depend on it. How a cohort with no coding background reaches that level is ADR-0009: students direct an agentic CLI they bring themselves, and the platform is the repository's harness configuration, not an application built on an agent SDK. The order in which they build it is ADR-0010: a process spine and a harness ladder, one element of each a week, starting from deliberate vibe coding.
  • A committed teaching mode for AIBS: a research module with a fixed thematic spine (ADR-0001), replacing Sketch 3's "4b" reading of AIBS as a taught strategy/economics/society course. The content survives; its function changes — every week's theme is a research assignment, not a lecture topic (Part 8).
  • The integrated learning-path system (Part 6) — the new contribution this LRD makes on top of both source documents: every team owns its weekly handbook contribution end-to-end, researching it and producing it through its own platform against a shared, instructor-set quality bar; progression is gated by an AI Socratic tutor and a mandatory cross-team peer partnership, without any bespoke adaptive-difficulty engine.
  • The decision trail. Seven tensions between the sketches and the proposal — teaching mode (ADR-0001), calendar (ADR-0002), module structure (ADR-0003), wiki grounding (ADR-0004), field research (ADR-0005), what AEL builds (ADR-0006) and individual assessment (ADR-0007) — are recorded in work/decisions/, and three further records follow them: the module names (ADR-0008), the AEL build environment (ADR-0009) and the AEL arc (ADR-0010). Each states a decision; all are Proposed. This LRD follows them and cites them where they bind, so a reader who disagrees with a choice here can go to the record rather than to the argument.

1.4 The AIBS vision — a research module

AIBS is designed by its owner as a research module. The proposal's own formulation of the assessment philosophy is the sentence every AIBS design choice in this LRD has to answer to:

In the age of GenAI, we are no longer impressed if you deliver content. We are impressed if you can show you have gained new insights and that you create value for companies.

What applied research asks of a student, in the proposal's terms, is six capabilities:

  1. make a sound problem analysis;
  2. understand what the client needs — the internal analysis;
  3. understand the context the client operates in — the external analysis;
  4. find access to reliable information and analyse it properly;
  5. do field research to gather new data;
  6. translate research insights into advice that can be applied.

Those six are the spine of AIBS's learning outcomes (§3.2). The handbook themes the proposal derives from them — the geopolitical field, the vendors' business models, whether the SME can trust AI (broadened from the proposal's data-safety question to its data, its output and its people, Part 8), what regional SMEs know and need to know, where their first opportunities lie, and which use cases have worked — are the thematic spine of the weekly programme (Part 8). Read in order they are one arc: the why — the forces shaping SMEs in Europe and the region, and whether AI can be trusted — then the current state — what Dutch and regional SMEs know, how they use AI today and what they expect of it, the IST against the SOLL — and finally the team's own independent view of what AI can do for an SME and what it will bring. And the working format it proposes — students as independent researchers in teams of four, a weekly contact block split between a plenary opening and per-team sprint reviews, one joint criterion-based interview in which both lecturers question from their own perspective — is carried into Parts 4, 6.7 and 9.

The proposal also raised four questions it did not settle: whether field research at a company can be required of everyone; whether the sister module still builds a business-case prototype, as students did last year, or the platform the handbook is written with; how a team's contribution stays recognisable once one handbook is assembled from all of them; and whether students should reflect at the interview on which company role needs which of the handbook's knowledge. Each is now a decision record — ADR-0005, ADR-0006 and ADR-0007 (the last covers both the separate submission and the role reflection) — and this LRD follows the decision on the table in each.

Part 2 — Pedagogical quality system

Four grounded frameworks (three in background-docs/education-design/; the fourth sourced from the AI Wiki) discipline every design choice in this LRD. They are stated here as design axioms, not summarised as literature.

2.1 Bloom's revised taxonomy — precision in what we ask for

Anderson & Krathwohl (2001) cross a six-level Cognitive Process Dimension (Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create) with a four-level Knowledge Dimension (Factual → Conceptual → Procedural → Metacognitive). A learning outcome is a verb (process) plus an object (knowledge type) — never a learning activity in disguise.

Design axiom: every learning outcome in Part 3 is tagged with its Bloom cell. A module that only asks students to describe or explain (Understand) is not doing what a capstone-driven minor needs; both modules here climb to Evaluate and Create, and — because the gating system in Part 6 explicitly asks teams to reflect on their own revision process — into metacognitive knowledge, the dimension classic rubrics rarely reach.

2.2 Motivation & Flow — why gates are calibrated, not arbitrary

Pink's Motivation 3.0 (Autonomy, Mastery, Purpose) operationalises Deci & Ryan's Self-Determination Theory; Csikszentmihalyi's Flow adds the experiential mechanism — a task must sit in the narrow channel between boredom (too easy) and anxiety (too hard), which maps directly onto Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development. Feedback must be informational ("here's how to improve") not controlling ("here's your grade") to sustain intrinsic motivation.

MasteryGates are binary and calibrated per week (Part 6.3) so a team is never blocked by a target it has no realistic path to — the checkpoint artifact scales with what that week's content actually taught, keeping teams inside the flow channel rather than the anxiety or boredom bands.

2.3 UnBlooms — grade the thinking, not the output

Austin (2026) argues that generative AI collapsed the assumption baked into every Bloom pyramid — that you must climb through Remember/Understand/Apply before you can Create. A student can now open an AI tool and skip straight to a polished artifact. Her response is not to invert the pyramid but to replace it with a metacognitive reflection loop — Questioning → Generating → Critiquing → Refining, organised around human agency and curiosity as the thing being protected, with "AI on the sidelines" providing productive friction through Socratic tutoring. The assessment question is never what did you produce; it is how deliberately did you think along the way.

Design axiom: this is the direct ancestor of the Socratic AI agent in Part 6.4. It is why that agent is contractually barred from ever telling a student their work is good, bad, or scored — verdicts short-circuit the reflection loop UnBlooms exists to protect. The loop's four stages (Questioning / Generating / Critiquing / Refining) map onto this LRD's cycle: the agent questions, the team generates a revision, a peer team critiques, the team refines — and only then does the gate open. This matters more, not less, now that the chapter itself is machine-generated (Part 6.1): a team's own agentic system can already reach Create in Bloom's terms without the team doing any of the underlying thinking — exactly the collapse UnBlooms describes. The gate is what forces the thinking back in. The AIBS owner's formulation of the same principle (§1.4) — insight and value, not content — is why AIBS's outcomes in §3.2 are research capabilities rather than topics.

2.4 Durable skills — the capability being protected

Globerson et al. (2026), synthesised on the AI Wiki's Durable Skills page, define a durable skill by three criteria: open-ended (not reducible to closed-form rules), socially/contextually situated (its performance depends on a specific group, organisation or domain), and slow-changing (it doesn't go obsolete every 18 months). The canonical cluster is Collaboration, Creativity and Critical Thinking — and Critical Thinking now explicitly includes AI-Supported Exploration and AI-Supported Verification as sub-skills: judging AI output is itself a durable skill, not a stopgap until the model gets better. McKinsey Global Institute's Skill Change Index (2025) corroborates from the labour-market side: judgment, negotiation and mentorship sit in the lowest-automation-exposure quartile, while narrow digital skills sit in the highest.

Design axiom: this is not a fourth independent framework bolted onto Bloom, Flow and UnBlooms — it is the answer to which capability those three are protecting. Every learning outcome in Part 3 is tagged with the durable skill it practises (§3.2–3.3), and the gate itself (Part 6.3) is reframed as weekly, structural practice of two specific sub-skills: AI-Supported Verification and Collaboration.

Part 3 — Learning outcomes (Bloom-tagged)

Part 4 — Target audience & prior knowledge

There is no discipline-specific admittance requirement — the minor is open to any student with at least three years of prior university study. Two populations naturally apply: business-oriented students preparing for an AI- and data-driven environment, and IT/Engineering students seeking deeper Machine Learning knowledge who also value the business modules. The design assumes the pairing: the default student takes both modules, holds one of the four build roles in AEL and the researcher role in AIBS, and is examined on both in one joint interview (ADR-0003). The modules are nonetheless formally separate, so a student may take one — a business-background student choosing AEL alone to learn to direct an agentic build, an engineering-background student choosing AIBS alone to build genuine research and business fluency — and the assessment has a single-examiner variant for that case (Part 9). Module choice is not a re-run of a student's degree; the programme encourages students to take the role that stretches them, and the pairing makes them take both.

Part 5 — Design criteria

5.1 KISS, stated as constraints

"Keep It Super Simple" is not an aspiration in this LRD — it is a set of things this system is not allowed to become, because each of the alternatives was considered and rejected during design:

  • No adaptive-difficulty algorithm. A tiered-branching or Socratic-depth-ladder system was considered and rejected in favour of a single binary gate per checkpoint — one mechanism, one rule, no scoring model to maintain or explain.
  • No student-visible scores. Students see open/locked and Socratic questions. Nothing numeric. This is a direct consequence of §2.3 (UnBlooms) as much as of KISS.
  • One team, not two paired teams. Researchers and builders — by default the same four students, wearing a different hat per module — work inside a single team producing a single artifact; not two role-split teams reviewing each other. A separate, single peer-review partnership (one other team, stable for the full run) is where structured outside feedback comes from instead (Part 6.5); rotating peer pools were considered, for exposure diversity, and rejected as unnecessary complexity.
  • One shared Socratic-agent component, instructor-owned, not built by anyone it grades. The module owners build and maintain it once (Part 6.4); every team consumes the same instance. No parallel, per-team or student-built tutoring systems, and no student control over the thing that gates them.
  • One shared rubric, not per-team custom criteria. Instructors publish one quality/design bar (Part 6.2) that every team's output is measured against — no bespoke grading standard to maintain per team.
  • Reuse existing infrastructure. The AI Wiki — a Quartz digital garden, LLM-maintained and human-curated — is the platform. This LRD adds a gating layer and a peer-review-partner registry on top of it — it does not stand up new shared infrastructure. Each team's own research platform is small, disposable, and theirs alone to run.
  • No handbook dependency on the platform. The research platform AEL builds (ADR-0006) is the team's tool, not the handbook's precondition: every step it automates keeps a manual route (FR-12), and the platform is judged on whether it works, not on whether the handbook came out of it. A tool a cohort cannot do without is a single point of failure, and a seven-week student build cannot be held to "must not fail".

5.2 What "manageable" means for a teacher

A single instructor dashboard (Part 6.6) must answer, for every team, in under a minute: where are they, is the gate open, has an override been used, does the peer feedback look genuine. If a teacher cannot answer those four questions in under a minute per team, the system has failed its own KISS test — regardless of how pedagogically elegant it is.

Part 6 — The integrated learning-path system

This is the new mechanism this LRD contributes. Every team runs the same production pipeline (6.1) against the same published bar (6.2), and can only advance through a gate (6.3) built from two checks: a Socratic AI tutor (6.4) and a cross-team peer partnership (6.5). An instructor dashboard (6.6) makes it all visible, and the weekly contact block (6.7) is where the theme is opened and the gates are talked through.

6.1 The team's research pipeline

Each week, a team turns the week's theme into one output: a handbook contribution. As AIBS, the team does the research — problem analysis, sources, field data, advice — and the team's own research platform is the agentic system it does it through: gathering and appraising sources, processing interview material, keeping citations honest, drafting the contribution and checking it against the shared criteria (§6.2). As AEL, the same team builds and tunes that platform, with its own researchers as the client (ADR-0006) — the users are in the room, and so are the requirements and the complaints. The one weekly checkpoint is the contribution itself.

The platform is the team's tool, not the handbook's precondition. For every step the platform automates, the team keeps a manual route (FR-12), and uses it whenever the platform is not there yet — which, under the asymmetric calendars (ADR-0002), is the normal state early on: the research needs the platform before the build has finished it. The platform is judged on whether it works; the contribution is judged on the research. The generated draft is where the two meet — the researchers vet what the platform produced before it counts as theirs, which is exactly what the Socratic gate (§6.4) probes.

This is deliberately different from the shared platform infrastructure (§1.2): every team builds and owns its own platform; no team runs another team's system.

6.2 Shared quality & design criteria

Before week 1, instructors publish one quality/design bar — the same criteria every team's output is measured against, no bespoke rubric per team. It reuses the handbook's chapter rubric (published on the handbook template: clarity, accuracy, responsible-AI coverage, usefulness to an SME owner) plus a matching design bar for the system itself (harness soundness, evaluation coverage). Two things happen with it:

  • The peer gate (§6.5) uses it directly — a peer team reviews this week's generated section against the published criteria, not against its own judgement of what's good.
  • A well-engineered team's own pipeline checks against it automatically, before submission — this is what AEL's evaluation-pipeline learning outcome (§3.3 #3) is for. Teams that skip this find out from their peer team instead, a week later.

Publishing the bar up front is itself a KISS move: it replaces "what does the instructor want" guessing with one fixed target, known from day one and unchanged mid-cohort (NFR-07).

6.3 Gated progression

Each teaching week has exactly one checkpoint artifact per team: that week's handbook contribution (§6.1) — six gates across AIBS's six teaching weeks. The consolidation week (ADR-0002) has no gate of its own: its output is the merged handbook, and the team's frozen submission (ADR-0007) is what the final Socratic and peer review feed into the interview. A team cannot start the next week's checkpoint until both conditions are met on the current one:

Gate conditionWho/what checks itWhat "met" meansDurable skill (§2.4)
A — Socratic gateThe Socratic AI agent (6.4)The team has submitted a written response to the agent's questions that is a genuine revision, not a resubmission — the hidden rubric's process dimension (not polish) clears a fixed threshold.Critical Thinking — AI-Supported Verification
B — Peer gateThe peer team (6.5)The team has both given and received one round of feedback with its peer team on this week's checkpoint, against the shared criteria (§6.2).Collaboration — Conflict Resolution, Project Management

Naming this matters: gate condition A drills the exact sub-skill — AI-Supported Verification — that the durable-skills literature (§2.4) flags as most at risk of atrophying without deliberate practice; gate condition B drills Collaboration's two most measurable sub-skills, every teaching week.

Both conditions, no partial credit, no numeric composite. The Socratic side is deliberately single-shot: one report per team per week (§6.4), not an unlimited resubmit-until-it-passes loop. If gate A doesn't clear on that one report, the team gets no second Socratic consult that week — the unresolved feedback carries into next week's submission alongside that week's new topic, and addressing it becomes part of what next week's report checks for. The peer gate (§6.5) has no such limit; a team can lean on its peer team as often as it likes between Socratic consults.

This is deliberately the "gated progression" option, not tiered branching or a depth ladder (§5.1) — the autoregression is in whether a team advances, not in which content it sees next. What varies between teams isn't how many times they can retry a single week — nobody can — it's how many weeks it takes to clear a carried-forward gate. The system rewards genuine week-over-week growth (§6.4), not speed or persistence.

A locked gate is not a penalty. It carries no grade consequence by itself (Part 9) — it is Pink's informational feedback, not controlling feedback (§2.2): a prompt to revise, not a mark against the team.

6.4 The Socratic AI agent

Owned by: the module owners/instructors — not students, not any team, not AEL's infrastructure build-group. The same people it evaluates cannot also control how it evaluates them; this is a deliberate exception to "AEL builds everything technical" (§1.2). Two outputs for two audiences — Socratic questions to the team, a rubric score to the instructor — and a gate condition, not only a pre-publication review step. It runs on a course-owned key with a hard spend cap, the one model-usage line the programme funds (ADR-0009).

  • The protocol: a team submits its checkpoint once and receives exactly one report per week — not an open-ended chat, not an unlimited resubmit-and-recheck loop. One submission, one report, once a week, per team.
  • To the team (visible): the report is Socratic questions only. Never a verdict, never "this is good/bad," never a score. Questions target the UnBlooms loop directly — why this topic and data, why this system design, and — now that the section itself is machine-generated — did the team critically vet what it produced, or just accept it.
  • To the instructor (visible): the exact same report the team received, plus a rubric score against the applied-research capability that week practises (AIBS, §3.2 and Part 8) or the harness/ADLC layer (AEL), plus the binary gate-A signal. Nothing is hidden from instructors — only from students.
  • Grows, or it isn't doing its job. Every report is generated with the team's full history of prior reports as context, under one standing instruction: never repeat a question that's already been answered, and never let a team coast on one that hasn't. A week's report that reads like a copy of last week's is a defect in the shared system, not an acceptable outcome — see Part 12 for how that's caught.

6.5 Cross-team peer network

Every team is paired, for the full run, with exactly one other team. This is the "force optimal use of the peer network" mechanism: the peer network is not self-selected or optional — it is a structural assignment, reviewed reciprocally every week. Because both teams in a partnership already contain both roles, the review itself is naturally holistic rather than split by lens.

What the peer team checksAgainst the shared criteria (§6.2)
Business feasibilityWould an SME owner trust and use this; does it fit the "first five steps" the chapter promises?
Engineering soundnessDoes the "Recommended tooling" section reflect what the team's own system actually generated and could build, at what effort?

Because every team has both kinds of expertise on hand, no review needs a translator — the peer team checks both angles in one pass, on one artifact.

Fallback. If a team's peer-review partner falls behind or drops the module, the instructor reassigns the partnership rather than blocking the other team (FR-08, Part 7) — mirrors the persona/archetype fallback for SME access (NFR-05).

6.6 Instructor dashboard

One screen, per team: current week, gate-A / gate-B status, this week's Socratic report and score, shared-criteria compliance from the last peer review, how many consecutive weeks (if any) gate A has carried forward, and an override control. The override exists because no binary gate should become a bureaucratic trap — an instructor can open a gate manually with one click, logged for the calibration record (Part 11, AC-07 bounds how often that should be needed).

6.7 The weekly contact block

Both modules share one weekly contact block of two and a half hours, and under ADR-0001 the week has two halves: the lecturer opens the theme plenary, and the teams then work as researchers inside it. The AIBS proposal offers two ways to fill the block. Both treat the per-team session as a sprint review — progress on the week's goals, what the team is stuck on, how it could be solved, the goals for next week — so that teams learn from each other's problems, not only from their own.

VariantShapeTrade
A — review slotsThree slots of forty-five minutes, three or four teams per slot. The lecturer runs a sprint review with those teams together and, depending on the week's theme, puts the same questions to every team to gauge what the week taught them.Maximum cross-team learning. No plenary, so the theme has to be opened in writing or inside the first slot — which sits awkwardly with ADR-0001's plenary opening.

The sprint review is where gate B's feedback is discussed and where a locked gate A (§6.3) gets its plan for the coming week — it is the human half of the gate system, not a third gate. Whether AEL shares the block or holds its own, and how the block sits in the timetable, is the timetable's call, not this document's.

There are two summative instruments, per ADR-0011, which supersedes ADR-0007: the criterion-based interview (CBI) at 70 % of each module's mark, and an individual portfolio built weekly across the run at 30 %. The CBI's shape follows the AIBS proposal's joint interview as ADR-0003 and ADR-0011 fit it to the examination regulations:

  • One joint interview, two marks (ADR-0003). Both lecturers conduct the interview together and each questions from their own perspective — technical and research. Each awards their own module's mark against their own criteria. The two criterion sets are written separately — not one rubric with a technical and a research column — and the procedure instructs scoring independently before comparing, so a strong technical answer is not marked up on research grounds or the reverse. A student taking one module gets a single-examiner variant.
  • Individual mark, team evidence (ADR-0011). The input is the handbook, the team's own contribution to it — submitted separately before the week-7 merge (FR-11) so that it stays recognisable after consolidation — and the student's own portfolio. Questions are put to the individual, not to the team; a student who contributed little to a good contribution can be marked down, and the criteria say so explicitly.
  • The individual portfolio (ADR-0011), 30 %. One document per student, added to every week, answering four fixed questions: what the team published, what this student contributed to it, what the gates asked and what the student changed as a result, and what their build role produced. It is continuous by construction — written in the week it describes, not assembled afterwards — and it is the instrument that makes the individual mark defensible without requiring the CBI to manufacture attribution in the room. Both lecturers read the same portfolio, each against their own module's criteria; there is one portfolio, not two. It also gives the adaptive run (`course-outline.md`) a written evidence base for deciding week N+1, which the design otherwise leaves to what was noticed in the session. Its risk is self-report: the portfolio makes a claim and the CBI tests it, so the criteria must state that an unsupportable large claim costs more than an honest small one.
  • The role-relevance reflection (ADR-0007, carried forward by ADR-0011). A standard question in every interview: which of the four build roles — product manager, developer, tester, deployer — needs which of the handbook's findings, and why. It tests transfer rather than recall, it connects the two tracks in the way the joint interview exists to, and it cannot be answered from a contribution the student did not help write. Who in a company needs which knowledge is also a handbook theme in its own right (Part 8, week 4). A student taking AIBS without AEL gets an alternative framing of the same question.
  • The fieldwork probe. Field research is a team obligation (ADR-0005), so a student can hold a research mark without having conducted an interview. The AIBS interview therefore asks each student specifically about the fieldwork — what was asked, what was found, what it changed — or the criteria stop claiming the skill (ADR-0007, carried forward by ADR-0011). The portfolio's contribution question makes the same gap visible weekly rather than only at the end.
  • Criteria still to be written, and now due twice over. ADR-0003 fixes that there are two sets; ADR-0011 fixes the instruments and their evidence base. Each set must now cover both the CBI and the portfolio: until they are published, 30 % of each mark rests on an unstated standard. The AIBS criteria are the AIBS owner's to write and the AEL criteria the AEL owner's, both before the shared quality bar is published (Part 13). This is now the most urgent piece of writing in the module.
  • A locked gate never itself changes a grade. It is a revision prompt, consistent with Pink's informational-feedback principle (§2.2).
  • The Socratic agent's hidden rubric score is an instructor calibration signal only — as in the original pre-publication review design. This LRD extends its use from "before publication" to "at every weekly gate," but does not change its evidentiary status. Instructors see the same report the team received, plus the score behind it (§6.4) — nothing about a team's weekly gate is opaque to the people grading them, only to the students being gated.
  • Two lenses, one shared history. In the AIBS half of the interview a student defends the research — the problem analysis, the sources and field data, the advice — and how critically they vetted what the team's platform produced; in the AEL half, the platform that produced it — the harness, the architecture choice, the eval coverage — walked through the team's own decision log: which failure each entry answers, and how the team's eval results support the trust claim its contribution makes (ADR-0010, Part 8 week 3). Both draw on the same exported gate/iteration history (FR-09), read through a different lens.
  • At the CBI, a student defends not just the contribution/platform but how they responded to the Socratic questions and to their peer team across the weekly gates — directly assessing the metacognitive-knowledge outcomes in Part 3 (AIBS #6, AEL #6).
  • The exported gate/iteration history (FR-09) is the evidence base for that defence — not a score sheet, a process trail.

  5. Stand up the peer-review-partner registry and dashboard (FR-06/07) alongside the week-1 pilot.

A.2 Current assessment criteria — the six CRISP-DM phases

The existing criteria are organised by CRISP-DM phase rather than by Bloom cell or durable skill. After completing the module, students can:

PhaseWhat students must demonstrate
1. Business Understanding
Decision context & business case		Clearly define a decision problem in a business context; identify stakeholders, decision-makers and organisational impact; formulate decision objectives and success criteria; explain how an AI-supported agent improves decision quality, efficiency or scalability; build a convincing business case and justify why AI is appropriate for the decision.
2. Data Understanding
Data requirements for the agent		Assess data availability, quality and limitations; explore how data supports decision variables, signals or predictions; reflect on data ownership and governance.
3. Data Preparation
Structuring data for decision support		Explain how data should be cleaned, structured or transformed; define the features, indicators or inputs the agent needs; address data-management issues such as consistency and reliability.
4. Modelling
Design of the AI-supported decision agent		Design the agent's architecture in a flow chart; explain the AI techniques or algorithms used or proposed; show how the model supports the decision-making process; design a human-in-the-loop system; justify model choice and complexity. Optional, for higher grades: build a working prototype and demonstrate the agent performing a simplified decision task.
5. Evaluation
Performance, risks, ethical reflection		Evaluate how well the agent supports decision-making; define evaluation metrics (accuracy, usefulness, business impact); identify limitations and uncertainties; reflect critically on ethical, privacy and data-security risks — bias, fairness, privacy, data security, responsible AI use, transparency and explainability; propose risk-mitigation strategies.
6. Deployment
Implementation & organisational integration		Describe how the agent would be implemented in an organisation; explain workflow integration and user interaction; identify organisational requirements (training, monitoring, maintenance); reflect on scalability and future improvements.

These criteria are not discarded by the redesign, but their role changes. The Socratic agent scores a AIBS team each week against the applied-research capability the week practises (§3.2, §6.4) rather than against a CRISP-DM phase. The six phases remain a useful map of the same ground — Business Understanding is the problem analysis and the internal analysis, Data Understanding and Preparation are the source and field work, Evaluation and Deployment are the translation into advice — and Part 3 restates what they ask for as cognitive level and capability, anchored to a professional role (§3.1) rather than to a report section.

A.5 Current assessment mechanics

Students prove their knowledge and capability through a single integrated professional task, assessed via a criterion-referenced interview (CBI) supported by three deliverables. Students may work individually or in pairs.

  1. Written report — structured by the six CRISP-DM phases; guideline six paragraphs, roughly 0.5–1 page each. Uploaded via HANdin. Deadline in the recorded cohort: Monday 30 March, 9:00.
  2. Architecture / prototype agent — a flow chart of the agent's architecture. A working prototype is optional, and required only for the highest grades (7–10).
  3. Presentation + CBI — a 20-minute pitch/demo, students choosing their own timeslot (recorded cohort: Monday 6 April); assessors Meike Botterweg and Oliver Ntenje. The CBI covers the business case and design, the CRISP-DM approach, and critical reflection on ethical and data-security risks. It is assessed on two components: a module grade and a CRISP-DM grade (20%).

Grading scale.

BandWhat it describes
0–2Insufficient — unclear problem, unrealistic business case, no coherent agent design, ethical issues not recognised.
3–4Limited understanding — problem identified but poorly justified, unclear architecture.
5.5–6Satisfactory to good conceptual design — clear business case, relevant data identified, clear architecture, human-in-the-loop considered, ethical risks recognised.
7–8Strong design with a working prototype — the above, plus a working simplified prototype.
9–10Excellent — deep understanding of business value, sophisticated human-in-the-loop design, critical ethical analysis, and a complex, well-functioning prototype beyond a proof of concept.

Two standing rules accompany the scale: attendance of at least 80% of classes is mandatory, and the principle repeated throughout the course — "a simple, well-designed agent with clear governance will score higher than a complex model nobody understands." That principle is the direct ancestor of §5.1's KISS constraints, applied in this LRD to the module's own machinery rather than only to the students' agents.

What the redesign keeps and what it changes. The CBI stays the sole summative instrument — now conducted jointly by both lecturers, with a separate mark per module (Part 9, ADR-0003) — and the prototype-versus-concept distinction that the 7–10 bands encode is what Part 3 resolves structurally instead: the build sits with AEL (§3.3), so a AIBS student is no longer graded partly on a capability the module tells them they do not need. What is added is formative, not summative — weekly gates (§6.3) that carry no grade of their own, and a process trail (FR-09) the CBI can draw on — plus, under ADR-0007, a separately submitted team contribution as the interview's evidence base. The grading scale above therefore remains in force; it is the route to it that changes.
