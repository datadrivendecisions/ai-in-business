AI in Business

Six weeks in which your team researches what a regional manufacturer needs to decide about AI — and builds the agentic platform that produces the answer.

You will spend six weeks on one question a real regional manufacturer is asking — should we be doing something with AI, and what? — and answer it properly: researched, published, and produced by a platform your own team builds.

This site holds the purpose, the structure, the assessment and the week-by-week programme for the two modules that run as one: AIBS and AEL. Week 1 is published; every later week is filled in about one week ahead.

Why this course

In September 2024 Mario Draghi handed the European Commission a report on why Europe is falling behind the United States and China. Its central finding is not that Europe lacks ideas. It is that Europe is slow to diffuse technology into the businesses it already has — and the place diffusion stalls hardest is the small and mid-sized firm. Fragmented markets, regulatory complexity, no capital to spare, no in-house skills, no scale. The gap in productivity is, to a large extent, a gap in adoption.

Most of those firms are within an hour's drive of this building. They are being told, loudly and from every direction, that they must "do something with AI". Very few of them have anyone who can tell them what, or whether, or first. This course puts you in that gap as a researcher who can find out and explain, not as a consultant.

Draghi's own address to the European Parliament — eleven pages, presenting the report in his own words — is your pre-reading for week 1. Eleven pages is enough; nobody needs to read all four hundred. The report itself is there if you want it.

What you will be able to do afterwards

Research

Turn a firm's vague worry into an answerable question

You take "should we do something with AI?" from a real regional manufacturer and turn it into a research question with sub-questions, answer it with desk and field research, and write the answer for someone who will act on it.

Judgement

Tell whether an AI output is any good

Most people arrive thinking the skill is producing text with a model. The scarce one is verification. You will practise it every week: you generate, then you check the claim, the source and the reasoning before you put your name on it.

Build

Direct an agentic system to do real work

You specify, build, test and deploy a working platform by instructing an agentic CLI rather than hand-writing code. You also learn what that way of working is good at, and where it fails without telling you.

Publish

Ship something a stranger reads

Your work is published, cited and read outside the classroom. It is a chapter of a handbook the next cohort builds on.

Where it sits, and what it assumes

AI in Business is the AI strand of HAN's minor Data Driven Decision Making in Business. It assumes what the minor already gives you: that you can frame a business problem, work with data and evidence, and defend a recommendation. It does not assume that you can program. In the technical track you direct an agentic command-line tool; the design rule is that a non-coder must be able to run it, and that every automated step keeps a manual fallback.

The course is taught, written and assessed in English. It does not assume you are Dutch, or that you already know this region and its firms. Several of you will not, and week 1 makes sure no team is short of that knowledge. Sometimes the work runs into Dutch: a partner who would rather be interviewed in it, a source that exists only in it. The team solves that together, and how a week runs says how.

What you make

One thing to make, approached from two sides.

The handbook

Your team writes a handbook about the state of AI, for SMEs: what a regional manufacturing owner needs to know and decide. Every week each team publishes one new page on that week's theme — a new page, never an edit to a previous one, so the trail of your thinking stays visible. In the assessment period the pages are consolidated and merged into one handbook.

Look at the handbook template — the shell every chapter fills, with the chapter anatomy and the quality bar each chapter is measured against.

The platform that produces it

Your team also builds the agentic platform you do that research and writing with: gathering and appraising sources, processing interview material, keeping citations accurate, drafting the page and checking it against the criteria. Your own researchers — the same four people — are its client.

The platform is your tool. The handbook never waits on it: for every step it automates you keep a manual route, and early on you will use it. The platform is judged on whether it works; the handbook page is judged on the research.

Two modules, one team

The two modules are taught as one pipeline, by both lecturers, to the same teams. AEL builds the tool; AIBS decides what the tool's output must achieve, and judges whether it does. They constrain each other on purpose: the research says what "good" means, the build says how you get there.

Teams of four, four build roles

Teams are formed in the first session and stay together for the run. Everyone researches in AIBS; in AEL each of you holds one build role, and the role is what you owe the team.

RoleWhat you owe the team
Product managerGuards the requirements: what the platform is for, what it must let a user do, what is deliberately out of scope. Owns the PRD.
DeveloperDrives the agentic CLI: turns the requirements into something that runs, and keeps a log of what failed and why.
TesterHolds the bar: checks the platform's output against the quality criteria before anyone else sees it, and says when it does not clear them.
DeployerGets the work published and reproducible: the page reaches the shared site, and someone else can run what you ran.

How to choose. Take the role you want to get better at, not the one you are already good at — none of the four is graded on how well you arrived at it. If you have never opened a terminal, developer is a legitimate choice and the tool is built on the assumption that you will make it. Everyone instructs the tool at some point; none of the four roles is a spectator, and at least one person on each team should be willing to drive the command line first.

The fifth job, which is not a role. Your team also has to reach a real firm, interview the people in it, and read sources about a region — and that work is not evenly easy for four different people. Whoever on your team can approach a local company, or read a Dutch source, is holding something the team needs. Share it out: do not hand it to one person, and do not let it stop anyone building. Teams that drift into the ones who talk to the firm and the ones who touch the tool produce the weakest handbooks, every time.

When you form a team, look for four people who do not already work together. Mix them: at least one who is comfortable at a command line, and at least one who knows this region or can work in Dutch. If your team has neither, say so in the session — we would rather fix that in week 1 than in week 4.

How a week runs

  1. One session a week, shared by both modules. Both lecturers, the whole cohort. The week's theme is opened, and then you work as a team inside it. Week 1 is a long one — about four hours; the shape of the later sessions is set as we go.

  2. Your team publishes one page. A new handbook page on that week's theme, on the shared site. New page, never an edit to an earlier one.

  3. Two gates, both informational. Neither is scored, and neither counts towards your mark. They exist to make you revise.

    They are called gates because your page passes through both before you call it done — not because either one can stop you. Nothing on this course is blocked by a gate: you never wait for permission, and no gate has to be cleared before you move on to the next week. A gate is two sets of questions arriving at a useful moment.

    • The Socratic gate — an AI tutor, run by the lecturers, questions your team's thinking. One submission per team per week; you get questions back, never a verdict and never a score.
    • The peer gate — a partner team reviews your page against the same published criteria everyone is measured against, and you review theirs.

  4. We decide the next week. What we saw in the session and in your pages determines what week N+1 asks for. That week's page on this site appears about a week before the session.

You add a page to your portfolio. Individually, every week, the same four questions — what the team published, what you contributed, what the gates changed, what your role produced. It is the one piece of the week that is yours rather than your team's, and it carries 30 % of both your marks. What goes in it.

Field research. Each team visits one regional SME partner, once, somewhere in weeks 2–4. It is a team obligation and the source of the field data your handbook pages rest on. Whether it runs exactly in that window is one of the things we may adjust.

Finding the partner is not your problem alone. The course maintains a pool of regional firms that have agreed to receive a team, and we match teams to it. If your team already has a contact you would rather use, you may — tell us first, so we can check the consent terms. In week 2 you are told which firm is yours, who to contact there, and what language the visit will run in, so you can plan the interview as a team.

The visit runs in whatever language the partner is comfortable in, and that is often Dutch. Plan for it as a team. An interpreter from inside your own team; a recorded interview your platform transcribes and translates; a partner who is happy to work in English. We will help you pick. What is not acceptable is a team in which two people conduct the research and two wait outside. Everyone must be able to work from the result, and everyone is questioned on it in the final interview.

How you are assessed

Two things carry your mark: a final interview, worth 70 %, and an individual portfolio you build a page at a time across the six weeks, worth 30 %. Everything else — the team's weekly pages, the two gates, the drafts — stays formative. It is the raw material your portfolio is written from, not the grade.

“The two gates”, here and below, means the two rounds of feedback your page gets every week. The Socratic gate: an AI tutor run by the lecturers questions your team's thinking. The peer gate: a partner team reviews your page, and you review theirs. Neither is scored and neither can stop you — how a week runs has them in full.

Your two marks are individual, and the portfolio is the reason they can be. Four people share a team page; only you can say what you did to it.

What it is
The instrumentsOne joint criterion-based interview at the end, in the assessment period — 70 %. Both lecturers conduct it together, each questioning from their own side. Plus your individual portfolio, built weekly and submitted once — 30 %.
The marksTwo independent marks, one per module. Each lecturer scores against their own module's criteria — a strong technical answer is not marked up on research grounds, or the reverse. Both read the same portfolio, each from their own side: there is one portfolio, not two.
The evidenceYour portfolio. Your team's frozen submissions — the pages as they stood before the merge. Your team's gate and iteration history: what the Socratic tutor asked, what your peer team said, and what you changed as a result.
What is not evidenceThe gates themselves — what the tutor asked, what your peer team said. Neither changes your mark, and neither is a verdict on your page; both are prompts to revise. What you did with those questions goes in your portfolio, and that is a different thing.
What you hand in at the endThe consolidated handbook, your team's frozen contribution to it, your team's platform together with the documents that specify it — starting with the PRD you draft in week 1 — and your own portfolio.

The portfolio

One document per student, yours alone, added to every week. It is short by design — about a page a week — and it always answers the same four questions:

  1. What did the team publish this week? The page, in a line or two.
  2. What did you contribute to it? Specifically, and without inflating it. "I wrote the section on vendor lock-in and found three of the six sources" is worth more than "I helped with the research".
  3. What did the two gates ask, and what did you change? The tutor's questions and your peer team's review. Not whether you agreed with them — what moved. A question you decided to reject, with a reason, counts as much as one you acted on.
  4. What did your build role produce? The PRD revision, the failure log, the test results, the deployment — whatever your role put out that week.

Write it the same week, not at the end. A portfolio assembled from memory in the assessment period is visible as one, and it is worth less than a thin record kept as you went.

An honest small contribution scores better than an unsupportable large one. The portfolio makes a claim; the interview is where the claim is tested. If you write that you built the evaluation pipeline, expect to be asked how it works.

What the interview is like. One conversation with your whole team and both lecturers, in English, in the assessment period. It is criterion-based: you are asked to account for choices your team made and evidence you produced — so it rewards knowing your own work, not performing. Each lecturer questions from their own side and marks their own module. Nothing about it advantages a native speaker of Dutch or of English; it advantages a student who can say why their team did what it did.

The two criterion sets — one per module — are written by each module's owner and published here before they are used to judge anything. Until they are on this site, they do not exist.

Fixed, and decided as we go

This course is run adaptively. We are not building six weeks of material in advance. We start with the first assignments, watch what you can already do, and decide week by week what comes next. That is deliberate: it lets us pitch the work at the level in the room instead of the one we guessed at in June. It has one consequence worth stating: some of what you want to know in week 1 does not exist yet in week 1.

So here is the split, and we will hold ourselves to it.

Fixed

Settled before week 1, will not move

  • The end goal: a handbook about the state of AI for SMEs.
  • The scope: six weeks of classes, then an assessment period.
  • Teams of four; one build role each; everyone researches.
  • One published page per team per week.
  • Two gates a week — the AI tutor and your peer team — both informational, never scored.
  • An individual portfolio, a page a week, carrying 30 % of each mark.
  • The finish: a consolidated handbook and one joint interview, two marks.

Self-assessment · week 1

The AI Worker Maturity Scale

Sixteen questions that place you on a six-level scale from Bystander to Compounding, and then name the single thing standing between you and the next level. Unlike most maturity models it measures a person, not a company, and it asks for observable facts about how you work rather than how capable you feel.

Use it before or during the first session. It is a sharper version of the confidence check we run in the room. The level it gives you, plus the one gate it says is holding you, is a good opening for your first portfolio entry.

It is not a validated instrument and it says so on its own front page: it is composed from published research rather than tested as a scale. Treat the result as a conversation with yourself, not a score. Your answers stay in your browser.

The maturity scale is reproduced here from the AI Wiki, where it is maintained and where its calibration record and sources live. It keeps its own look on purpose — it is a tool that happens to live on the course site, not a page of the course.

Practical

When it runs
Six Mondays: 7, 14, 21 and 28 September, 5 and 12 October 2026, then the assessment period, which follows week 6 in the minor's own calendar. Room and start time are in your timetable. Your team's interview slot, and the assessment-period dates, are published on this page in week 5 — a fortnight's notice. If you have a hard constraint on when you can be here, tell us in week 4, not week 6.
Who teaches it
Meike Botterweg — AIBS, the research track. Witek ten Hove — AEL, the technical track. Both are in every session.
If you are stuck
Ask in the session: the team work time is for exactly this, and both lecturers circulate through it. Between sessions, mail the lecturer whose side the problem is on, or bring it to the start of the next session; if you are unsure which side it is, ask either. Being stuck early is ordinary and we would rather hear it in week 2 than in week 6.
Where the work goes
One document per team, per assignment. The default deadline is the start of the next session — so week 1's drafts are due by Monday 14 September. If a week needs a different deadline, that week's page says so and says it a week ahead; the default never moves later without notice. Where to put the documents is named at the close of each session and repeated on that week's page.
When this site changes
Each week's page appears about a week before its session. Nothing already published is rewritten without saying so.
What to do first
Read week 1 and the Draghi address it links. That is all the preparation the first session needs.
