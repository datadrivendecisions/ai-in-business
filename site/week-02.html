Week 2 — who owns the intern

The week the machinery starts. Your team publishes its first handbook page and hands in its first blueprint. And you find out what the tools you rely on will confidently tell you about their own industry.

Everything you need for the second session is on this page: what to bring, what happens in the room, and the assignments in full. Two are for AIBS; AEL asks for one document.

Before you come

When and where
Monday 14 September 2026. Room and start time are in your timetable — the same as week 1. This is the second of six Mondays.
Read
Three things, all short. This website, top to bottom. Draghi's speech to the European Parliament, linked from week 1. And the AI maturity ladder: decide where your target SME sits on it, because you will be asked.
Bring
A laptop, and an AI tool you can actually sign into. Any tool, any vendor. You will use it in the room for about twenty minutes, so this is the one thing not to leave until Monday morning. It does not have to be the same tool as your teammates — it is better if it is not.
Also bring
Your team's two week-1 drafts, the research proposal and the PRD. You will be working from both, particularly the target SME you named.
Do
Make sure your first portfolio entry is written. This week's entry builds on it, and an entry assembled from memory at the end of the run reads like one.

Week 2's theme is geopolitics and vendor models: who builds the AI systems a regional SME would actually use, who owns those firms, where they sit, and what changes for that SME if the arrangement shifts. It follows directly from the Draghi discussion in week 1 — that conversation was about Europe's position; this one is about what depends on it.

What happens in the session

About four hours with one break, in one room, with both lecturers. Timings are approximate. The session has three jobs. Go through what week 1 produced. Put the week's theme in front of you as something you make rather than read. And introduce the document AEL asks for this week.

The slides shown in the session are on one page: week 2 slides.

  • 0:00–0:20

    Before we start: any questions?

    The website, Draghi's speech, the maturity ladder. Where does your target SME sit on the ladder, and what would move it one rung?

  • 0:20–0:50

    Your research proposals and PRDs

    Both drafts are in and both were read. For the proposals: sub-questions per theme that map onto the internal and the external analysis. A structure that reads as one plan. And the link forward to the handbook and the tool. For the PRDs: the three things almost every draft missed, on one slide. You will revise both; nothing from week 1 was final.

  • 0:50–1:30

    Assignment 1, in the room — report writing with AI

    Three prompts, three separate conversations: ask an AI tool to argue that the United States dominates AI. Then, in a new conversation, that China does. Then Europe. Do not cross-reference; each article is written as if it were the only one. Then read the three back to back and note what you notice.

    The point is not that AI is bad at this. It is that you get what you asked for: a convincing US case, a convincing Chinese case, a convincing European case. Source diversity, not a cleverer prompt, is what tells you who leads.

  • 1:30–1:45

    Takeaway — AI braindump, or human-reviewed?

    We look at every assignment this way, and so should you. Make it visible in your own work: what did you write, what did AI draft, and what did you check?

  • 1:45–1:55

    Break

    Before you go: set AI to work on something. Rule 1 of the rulebook.

  • 1:55–2:25

    AEL — the technical blueprint

    Your PRD is the first of six documents; the blueprint is the second and it is this week's. What a blueprint is: the parts of your platform, what passes between them, and the line nothing may cross. And how it is read beside your PRD from now on.     Way of working — partner teams, the Socratic tutor, the rulebook

    Every team pairs with one other team and reads what it publishes; that pairing holds for the run.     Each team, one sentence: what is the first part of our platform, and what passes out of it? And one open question. Then what goes in your second portfolio entry.

The assignments

From this week on, the shape is the same every week. One published handbook page per team on the week's theme, and one AEL document posted in your team's channel. This week AIBS adds two assignments that feed the page, one done in the room and one after it.

AIBS — assignment 1: report writing with AI

Done in the session. Three articles from three separate conversations — the US dominates AI, China does, Europe does — written without cross-reference and read back to back.

  1. Keep all three. They are evidence for assignment 2 and for your page: the shape a confident, one-sided article takes.
  2. Note what you noticed. Three or four lines: what the three agree on, where they contradict, and what none of them cites.

The bar: you can say, from your own three articles, why "ask AI who leads" is not a research method.

AIBS — assignment 2: search strategies and quality criteria

The question: good-quality reports on the geopolitics of the AI race — the United States, China, Europe.

  1. Your criteria, explicit. What makes a source "good quality" for this question? Write the list down before you search, not after.
  2. Your strategy, explicit. How did you search, where, and why? The strategy is part of the hand-in.
  3. Then AI, carefully. A first analysis of the sources you found, with AI, and with the strategy you gave it made explicit too.

The bar: another team could repeat your search from your write-up and land on sources of the same quality.

AIBS — the handbook page: geopolitics and vendor models

One page of your team's handbook, written for the SME you named in your research proposal, answering the question that owner would actually ask: who owns this thing, and what happens to me if that changes? It follows the chapter template and meets the four criteria on it. Every claim carries an appraised source — who says it, and one line on why they are worth believing. The three articles from assignment 1 are evidence, not content. It ends in something the owner can do.

Scope and depth are confirmed in the session.

The bar: an owner reading it knows what they are depending on and what they would do if it changed.

AEL — the technical blueprint

The second of the six documents. It implements your PRD: one to three pages, boxes and words rather than code. The developer holds the pen; the whole team reads it, because the promises it has to carry are the PRD's.

Before you start, work through Draw your platform as a team. It covers the shapes a platform like yours can take, and ends in six decisions that become the first draft of this document.

  1. The parts. What each part of your platform does, in one sentence each. For a research platform a part is a step on the path from a source to a published page: add, appraise, translate, draft, check, publish. The first part most teams draw is the context file the platform always reads: your target SME, your quality criteria, what is out of scope.
  2. What passes between the parts. A file with a fixed shape is the contract between two parts, and it is what lets two of you build two parts at once. Name the file and its shape for at least one seam.
  3. The line nothing may cross. One sentence on where it runs and what keeps it there. Ours: interview material never reaches a model service. Yours will have an equivalent.
  4. The trace to the PRD. Every promise in the PRD lands in a part, or you say why not. A departure is allowed if the blueprint names it — and then the PRD is revised, with a version, a date and one line of reason.

The criteria the blueprint is read against are published here before the deadline, as the PRD's were.

Done when a reader can tell: what each part does, what passes between the parts, and where the line is that nothing may cross.

How they fit together, this week

The blueprint is only as good as the PRD it implements, and the PRD is only as good as the research proposal behind it. If drawing the parts is hard because your team cannot agree what the platform is for, that is not a blueprint problem. It is the PRD telling you it is still vague. Fix the PRD, and say in the blueprint that you did.

The same holds for the page: a quality criterion your platform cannot check is a criterion you will not apply consistently for six weeks. Assignment 2 makes you write the criteria down; the blueprint makes you say which part checks them.

Hand-in

What
One published handbook page per team, the technical blueprint, assignment 2's write-up — criteria, strategy, first analysis — and one portfolio entry per person.
Who
The page, the blueprint and the write-up are the team's. The portfolio is yours alone, and nobody else writes in it.
Where and when
Due by the start of the next session — Monday 21 September. The page is handed in by being published on the shared site. The blueprint goes in your team's Teams channel: the file itself, or a link to a single page on GitHub. A whole repository or a Google Doc cannot be read and counts as not handed in. Where assignment 2's write-up goes is said in the session.
The gates
Your page goes to your partner team. Your blueprint goes to the Socratic tutor. It reads it beside your PRD and sends back three to five questions in the same channel. Your next document is expected to include a section that returns to the open ones. Neither gate is scored. What you changed because of them goes in your portfolio, and that is assessable.

The page and the blueprint are not marked. They are evidence for your final interview — see how you are assessed. Your portfolio is the part that carries a mark: 30 % of both your marks, and this is the second of six entries.

Your second portfolio entry

This is the first entry using the four standard questions, because for the first time there is a published page and a document the tutor has read. About a page:

  • What did your team publish? One or two sentences on the page and what it argues.
  • What did you contribute to it? Named and specific. An honest small contribution is worth more here than an unsupportable large one — the portfolio makes the claim and the interview tests it.
  • What did the gates ask, and what did you change? Not whether the gate was satisfied. What moved, and what you decided not to change and why.
  • What did your build role produce? The PRD revision, the blueprint, the part you named, the search strategy — whatever your role actually put out this week.

After week 2

Field research opens. One regional SME partner per team, planned across weeks 2 to 4 — so this is the point to start arranging a visit rather than the point to start thinking about one. Nobody approaches a company before the consent and anonymisation text is signed off; that is a hard rule, and interview material never goes through a free-tier AI tool.

The week map has week 3 as trust and AI safety, with the knowledge architecture as the third of the six documents. As always, that page appears here about a week before its session, and what it contains depends partly on what this week shows us.
