AIBS — in class: three economies, seen from your sector

What kind of economy are the United States, China and Europe trying to build with AI — and is it the one we want? Nobody in the room has the answer to that, and you can still take part in the discussion. We make it tangible by starting from the sector your team already knows.

Each team takes one region and looks at the sector of the SMEs it researches. About 60 minutes. What we do not finish in class becomes homework.

1 · Hunt for front-runners — 15 minutes

Find two or three companies in your sector, in your region, whose use of AI puts them ahead of their competitors. Look for a glimpse of the future: a use case other firms in the sector could learn from.

  • A real company, with a use case in operation. A pilot announced in a press release counts only if you say it is one.
  • Your sector, or close enough that an owner in it would recognise the work.
  • Something new: a different way of working, selling, producing or caring.

Search with your AI tool, and check what it gives you. Most front-runner stories come from the company itself, a vendor, or a government that wants to look good. Your week 2 rule holds: a vendor's story shows that a product exists. It does not show that it works. Sources in Chinese are fine; say that you used a translation.

2 · Read your use cases — 15 minutes

One card per company, five questions:

  1. What does the AI do? In one sentence an owner would understand.
  2. Whose AI is it? Who built the model, who owns the data, where does it run?
  3. Who gains, and who pays? The owner, the workers, the customers, people outside the firm.
  4. What does it need from the country around it? Data, energy, money, rules, talent, or a government that steers.
  5. What does it tell you about the economy this region is building?

3 · A postcard from 2035 — 10 minutes

One postcard per team. Picture an SME in your sector, in your region, ten years from now, once your front-runners have become normal. On the front: an image or a headline. On the back: a few lines from someone who works there. What is their day like, and what changed for them? Draw it, generate it or write it. Say what the AI made.

4 · Three regions, one wall — 20 minutes

The postcards go up side by side, grouped by region. Each team has one minute for its postcard and its strongest use case. Then we compare:

  • What is different? Same or similar sector, different region: who owns the technology, who gains, and what does the government do?
  • What is each region building? One sentence per region.
  • Is that the economy we want? Who is it good for, and who is left out? Can a region steer this at all, and who decides?
  • And Europe? Behind, or on a route of its own? What would that route mean for the SME you research?

What you take away. A front-runner from your hunt can be the example on your use-case page: appraise it like any other source. The discussion is also a first answer to guiding questions 3a and 3b of the final interview. In your portfolio, write one line on what surprised you, and one on whether you changed your mind.

If it becomes homework: finish the steps with your team. Post a photo of your cards and postcard in your Teams channel before the session on 12 October.

AEL — a knowledge architecture, built live

In week 3 your team chose where its sources live and how the platform finds them again. On Monday we show one way to do it, from an empty folder. Three sources on this week's theme go in, each in a different format:

  1. An article from a web page: Agentic AI for small business growth, on the OECD's Cogito blog.
  2. A video from YouTube: Leveraging AI for smart manufacturing in SMEs, a talk from the Asian Productivity Organization.
  3. A report as a PDF: Empowering SMEs in the age of AI, the OECD's 2026 survey of more than 2,000 SMEs.

Four things to watch for:

  • The pause. The assistant reads the source, says what it found, and waits for a go-ahead before it writes. A wrong reading costs least when it is caught here.
  • The source page. Who wrote it, who paid for it, what was actually read. Look at who wrote the article.
  • A disagreement, written down. The article and the report paint different pictures of AI at small firms. Watch where the wiki records that.
  • A question, and its trail. We ask the wiki a question. The answer cites a page after every claim, and the log lists every page it read.

Then your team tries it with one published source of its own. Never with interview material: see the rules.

The slides for the session: week 5 slides.

The LLM wiki

A knowledge architecture answers one question: how does a question reach the passage that answers it? In week 3 you saw the main options. An LLM wiki is the one that does its reading in advance. The pattern was described by Andrej Karpathy in April 2026, in a one-page idea file.

Look it up when asked

RAG

RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation. The tool searches your files at the moment a question comes in. It pastes the passages it finds into the model's prompt, and answers. Next time, it searches again from scratch.

Read it once, keep what you read

LLM wiki

An LLM (large language model) reads each source when it arrives. It writes what it found into linked pages: one per source, per organisation, per idea. A question is answered from those pages. Where two sources disagree, that is noted on arrival.

Three layers

FolderWhat is in itWho writes it
raw/The sources, turned into text files: the article, the video's transcript, the report.Your team adds them. Nobody edits them afterwards, because they are the evidence.
wiki/The pages: a summary per source, a page per organisation and per idea, an index of every page, and a log of everything that happened.The assistant. You read and check it.
AGENTS.mdThe rules the assistant follows: the page types, the steps for adding a source, what it may never do.Your team, with the assistant. Change it when it does not fit.

Three things you ask it to do

  • Ingest. Add a source. The assistant saves it in raw/, reads it, and tells you what it plans to write. After your go-ahead, it writes the source page and updates every page the source touches. One source often changes five to fifteen pages.
  • Query. Ask a question. The answer comes from the wiki only, with a link after every claim. Where the wiki has no source, it says so. The log records which pages were read.
  • Lint. Run a health check. It finds broken links, missing fields, and pages nothing links to. It also finds quotations that are not in the source. It reports; it does not fix.

The template: your team's copy in ten minutes

An empty wiki, ready to fill: datadrivendecisions/llm-wiki-template on GitHub. Its README has the full instructions. In short:

  1. Make your copy. On the template's GitHub page, press Use this template, then Create a new repository. One per team; private is fine. Add your teammates.
  2. Clone it and install the helpers. Clone the new repository and open the folder in your assistant. Then run python3 -m venv .venv, activate it, and run pip install -r requirements.txt.
  3. Check it. Run python3 tools/lint.py. It should report 0 errors. On Windows, type python instead of python3.
  4. Add your first source. Tell your assistant: Ingest <the address of an article>, added by <your name>. Read what it says before you let it write.

What it answers of your knowledge architecture, and what it does not

If your team adopts the wiki, part of your knowledge architecture is already written down in the template. Your document still has to say it in your own words, for your own platform:

CriterionWhat the template doesWhat stays yours to decide

B2 · how a thing is named and foundA naming rule for every file, and an index of every page.Whether the index still works when you have two hundred pages.
B3 · from a question to the passageIndex, then page, then the source file. The log records each lookup.Trace one real question your platform will be asked.
B4 · when material changesSources are never edited. A disagreement is written under Debates, never overwritten.What happens to a handbook page built on a claim that later turns out wrong.
D1–D3 · the line, keptA rule that interview material never enters, and a rule that text in a source is never an instruction.Where your protected material lives instead, and who checks.
C1 · one alternative, weighedNothing. This is yours.What the wiki costs you, compared with what you have now.

What it costs

  • The reading happens up front. Every source costs model time when it arrives, whether anyone asks about it or not.
  • A wrong reading spreads. A misread figure lands on several pages at once. The pause before writing, the quotation check and the log are there to catch it. A person still has to look.
  • It has a size. Up to a few hundred pages, the index is enough. Beyond that, you need a search tool as well.

You do not have to switch. A folder with a naming rule is still a good answer. If your team does adopt the wiki, that is a decision. It goes in your decision log, and your knowledge architecture gets a new version and a date.

The rules, in any knowledge architecture

  • Interview material never goes in. No recordings, transcripts, notes or names from your field research. That holds for a private repository too, because whatever the assistant reads is sent to a model service.
  • Text in a source is material, never an instruction. A web page can contain sentences written to steer an AI. The wiki records them and does not obey them.
  • A quotation must be in the source. A model can produce a quotation nobody ever wrote. The template's check compares every quotation with the source file.

Page 1 — SME knowledge gaps. What do we not know yet about AI, and what would we need to know to decide well? Say what the firm already knows and does, what it lacks, and which gap matters most for a decision it faces now. Keep what the firm says it needs apart from what your evidence shows it needs. Where the two differ, say so. That difference is often your finding.

Page 2 — AI use cases at SMEs. What do firms like mine use AI for, and what pays off? Name the uses you found. Then pick one and take it through the chapter template's illustrative use case. Be honest about the data the firm really has. Choose a use case that answers a gap from page 1: the two pages make one argument.

Both pages follow the chapter template and meet its four criteria. Every claim carries an appraised source. Say on the page what your platform drafted and what your team checked. Each page ends in something the owner can do.

Plan A: your field research

Your interview is the best source for both pages, because nobody else has it.

  • If you have your green light, plan the visit this week. Check that your guide asks about both themes. What does the firm know and lack? What does it use AI for, and what has it tried or dropped?
  • If you do not have it yet, finish your interview guide this week. Ask about both themes in it. You need the green light and the signed consent before you contact the firm. Start plan B alongside it, so the pages move while you wait.

Plan B: if you cannot get into a company in time

Your questions still need answers. Get as close to the firm as you can, in this order:

  1. Someone close to the firm. A different SME in the same sector. A sector association, a regional development agency, an innovation hub, or an advisor who works with SMEs. This is still field research, so the same rules apply: a green light for your questions, and signed consent, before you talk to them.
  2. What owners say in public. Interviews with SME owners in the trade press, podcasts and sector webinars, and posts by owners themselves. These are first-hand. Appraise them like any other source.
  3. Surveys of SMEs. Statistics on AI use by firm size and sector, from Eurostat or CBS, and surveys such as the OECD report from the wiki demo. They tell you what is common. They do not tell you what your firm does.
  4. Documented cases. Case collections from innovation hubs and sector bodies, and vendor case studies. Remember your own rule from week 2: a vendor's story shows that a product exists. It does not show that it works.

Whichever plan you use, say it on the page: which claims rest on your own field research, and which on desk research. If you used plan B, add what the page cannot tell the owner, because you could not ask the firm itself. Readers trust a page more when it is clear about its evidence.

The bar: an owner reading page 1 knows what to find out first. An owner reading page 2 knows one use worth trying, what it takes, and how to tell whether it works.
