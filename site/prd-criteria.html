What a good PRD looks like

A reader can tell what you're building, for whom, and how you'll know it works.

That single sentence is the bar the week 1 assignment sets. This page is that bar unpacked into the twenty things a reader looks for, so you can hold your own draft against them before anyone else does.

The criteria come from Marily Nika's Building AI-Powered Products (O'Reilly, 2025), folded into the six sections the assignment asks for, plus a seventh group for the document as a working instrument. Each criterion says where it comes from.

This is published before it is used. Your PRD is read against these criteria and nothing else. Where the book and the brief pull in different directions, the brief wins — the three places that happens are named below.

Three departures from the book

Nika's template is written for a nought-to-one commercial AI product with a market. Your platform is an internal research tool whose user is your own team, so three parts of the template are deliberately trimmed.

The user is the team

"Customer segments" and "personas" become the four researchers in their roles, with the SME owner as the downstream reader of what the platform produces. A PRD that answers who with an SME has drifted back to a client prototype.

No market, no rollout

There is no market to size and no launch to plan. What survives is the technology analysis — what exists already, the wiki, the site, the CLI — and stakeholders and communication: who sees what, when.

Architecture is one paragraph

The technical blueprint is week 2's artefact. Nika wants it "on a very high level"; here a data-flow sentence is enough, and a diagram is not required.

The twenty criteria

Five groups follow the assignment's six sections; the last group, G, is about the document itself.

A · Problem and users

  • A1 — Problem statement, in one piece. One statement in Nika's form — who spends too much time on, or cannot do, what, with what consequence — and a tl;dr the reader meets first. Template §1, §3
  • A2 — Users named concretely and kept apart. The team as researchers, by role and by what each does with the platform in a week; the SME owner as the reader of the output, with what they need from a page. The two are distinguished, and the team is the primary user. Template §2 personas
  • A3 — Pain points carried by evidence. The problem is grounded in the AIBS problem analysis and the week-1 spike's failure log, quoted or pointed at, so a reader can see the users are bothered enough. Not a hunch. Ch. 2 dos and don'ts
  • A4 — Why an agentic platform, specifically. Name what the agent does that a wiki plus hands does not, and what it would be unwise to hand it. The "shiny AI object" test: would the argument survive if the technology were not interesting? Template §3, §4; ch. 2 don'ts
  • A5 — Hypothesis, falsifiable. "By bringing x to life, z becomes easier, faster or more reliable for the researcher" — stated so that a week's use could show it wrong. Template §3

B · What it must let a user do

  • B1 — The path, as a user journey. From raw source material to a published handbook page, step by step, with who acts at each step: where the agent acts, where a person does, and where a person checks. Template §5; assignment §2
  • B2 — Functional requirements say what smart behaviour, and why. What the platform must do and why that needs an agent — not which model, which prompt library or which framework. Template §5
  • B3 — Prioritised, with the uncertainty named. Which capabilities are the minimum and which come later, with a reason, and confidence made explicit where feasibility is unknown. A flat list of everything scores low. Ch. 2 step 4; template §4
  • B4 — Sources and data. What source material the platform ingests, in what form, where it comes from, where it and the drafts live, and what may not be fed to it. Template §5, §6; ch. 7 privacy

C · Output qualities

  • C1 — A good page, in checkable terms. State the qualities of a handbook page so a reader could hold a page against them and answer yes or no per quality: sourced at the claim, sources appraised, addressed to an SME owner who acts on Monday. Adjectives without a test — clear, useful, professional — score low. Template §8
  • C2 — Good enough to publish: the threshold. A minimum viable quality: what must be true of a page before it goes up, and who decides, with the reasoning — what the team expects of itself, what the reader risks if it is wrong. Ch. 3 MVQ; template §8
  • C3 — How the team will know the platform works. A small blend, not a single number: one North Star; at least one product measure (pages on time, hours per page); at least one AI-specific measure (unconfirmable citations per page); and one guardrail — the harm you watch for, such as fluent prose nobody checked. Each measurable with what you have, on a stated cadence. Ch. 6; template §8

D · Known constraints

  • D1 — The four given constraints, owned. Each of the brief's constraints — directing an agentic CLI rather than hand-coding; reusing the wiki and site; runnable by a non-coder; a manual fallback for every automated step — restated in your own words with its consequence for the design. A copy of the four lines scores 1. Assignment §4; template §4
  • D2 — Risks, each with a mitigation or a detection. Risks specific to an AI research platform: fabricated or mismatched sources; confident prose nobody read; drift from the research question; cost or time per page. Each with what you will do about it, or how you will notice. Non-functional requirements you add beyond the given four belong here too. Template §4, §5; ch. 5

E · Out of scope

  • E1 — Explicit exclusions with a reason. What you are deliberately not building, one line of reason each, and where useful when it might return. "No UI" without a why scores 1. Ch. 3 scoping
  • E2 — Scope proportionate to seven weeks and a non-coding team. The minimum you promise is something you can have working, badly, by week 2 and improve weekly. A PRD promising a trained model, a full pipeline, or a performance level you cannot yet know scores low. Ch. 4

F · Open questions and assumptions

  • F1 — Assumptions marked as assumptions, with a consequence. Each assumption is labelled as one and says what changes if it is wrong. Template §4; worksheet 1
  • F2 — Open questions that are real, owned and routed. Not rhetorical. Each has an owner and a route to an answer — which week, which experiment, which conversation — and says what it will gain when answered. Template §6; ch. 5

G · The document as a working instrument

  • G1 — Header and provenance. Product name; the product manager as author; contributors named by role; a version and a date; links to the relevant documents — the AIBS problem analysis, the week-1 failure log, the wiki or site it reuses. Template header
  • G2 — The three-question test. From the document alone, in a minute each: what are you building, for whom, and how will you know it works. This is the assignment's own bar and the only holistic item. Assignment; template §1

What is not read against you

Not scored, on purpose: length beyond the one to three pages; prose quality; the presence of Nika's headings as such; and anything about the platform's code, which no one has yet.

Nor is anything the brief does not ask for — market analysis, competitor analysis, rollout strategy, architecture diagrams. Brevity is not penalised either: a one-page document that answers the three questions beats three pages that do not.

One thing is worth more than it looks. An honest gap, named in the open questions with an owner and a route, is worth more than a filled section that asserts.
