What a good blueprint looks like

A reader can tell what each part of the platform does, what passes between the parts, and where the line is that nothing may cross.

That sentence is the bar the week 2 assignment sets. This page unpacks it into the twenty things a reader looks for. Hold your own draft against them before anyone else does.

The criteria come from three places. The first is the four things the assignment asks for. The second is the six decisions at the end of Draw your platform. The third is the checks professional architects run on each other's drawings. Each criterion says where it comes from.

Posted already? Nothing is lost. If you post a second version before the deadline, the latest one is read. Your blueprint is read against these criteria and nothing else.

Three things that are different here

Professional architecture documents are written for large systems with many readers. Yours is one to three pages for a team of four, so three things are trimmed or added.

Short on purpose

No lists of everyone with an interest, no server diagrams, no code. Google's engineers write "mini design docs" of one to three pages for work of this size. A drawing, a sentence per part and a few decisions are enough.

A part can be a person

In ordinary software every part is code. In yours, a part can be a model, a script or a person with a checklist. Saying which one it is, is part of the design.

Parts can be confidently wrong

A model gives a wrong answer in the same fluent prose as a right one. The architecture books do not cover this, so group D asks who checks the work and what happens when it is wrong.

The twenty criteria

Groups A to C follow the assignment's first three asks. D covers the decisions about control and failure from the tutorial. E is the trace to your PRD. F and G are about the document itself.

A · The parts

  • A1 — Each part's job, in one sentence. Every part has a name and one sentence that says what it takes in and what it hands on. If the sentence needs an "and", it is two parts. A box labelled only with a tool, such as "Claude" or "Python", has a name but no job. Assignment ask 1; C4 model notation
  • A2 — What does the work: code, a model or a person. For each part, say which one does the job. Where a model does something a simple script could do, say why. Draw your platform, "Words on this page"; Anthropic, Building effective agents
  • A3 — The shared context. Where your target SME, your quality criteria, what is out of scope and your house style live. One named place, and which parts read it. Draw your platform, decision 4

B · What passes between the parts

  • B1 — Every arrow says what it carries. Each arrow on the drawing is labelled with the thing handed over, such as a file, a list of claims or a draft. It points one way. "Uses" or "sends" is not a label. If colours or shapes mean something, a key says what. Assignment ask 2; C4 model notation
  • B2 — One seam, one contract. For at least one seam: which part hands to which, the file's name, and the fields in it. Someone could write that file by hand if the part before it broke. Assignment ask 2; Draw your platform, decision 3
  • B3 — The drawing and the text tell the same story. The same parts, with the same names, in both. Nothing appears in only one of them. Clements et al., Documenting Software Architectures
  • B4 — A claim keeps its source. The path shows how a claim on the published page stays linked to the source it came from, also through translation. It names the part that checks that link. Week 2: every claim needs a source you would defend

C · The line nothing may cross

  • C1 — The line, in one sentence. In the form "X never reaches Y", about your own material: a named kind of data and a named place it may not go. "We follow the GDPR" is not a line. Assignment ask 3; Draw your platform, decision 5
  • C2 — Where the line is held. The place the line is checked, drawn on the drawing: one door, one part, or a person who carries the material by hand. Say what keeps it there. A line written only in a sentence is checked nowhere. Draw your platform, decision 5; threat modelling (trust boundaries)
  • C3 — What goes out through the door. For each part that calls a model service: what it sends, and to which service. Every flow to the outside is drawn, so a reader can check that nothing crosses the line unseen. Threat modelling (data-flow diagrams); European Data Protection Board on LLMs

D · Who is in charge, and being wrong

  • D1 — Who decides what happens next. One part, a person, or parts reacting to events, named. The drawing also says which altitude it shows — the production line, the company, or both — and who owns which stage. Draw your platform, section 3 and decision 2
  • D2 — Who checks the work before it is published. Which part or person holds a page against the quality criteria in your PRD, at which point on the path. Draw your platform, decision 6; week 2: "the blueprint makes you say which part checks them"
  • D3 — What happens when a part is wrong or fails. Does the work stop, go back a stage, or reach a person? How many tries before it stops? Silence is also an answer: the one that publishes the mistake. Draw your platform, section 5 and decision 6
  • D4 — The manual fallback. For each automated part, how a person does the same job by hand without the rest of the platform noticing. Course constraint; Draw your platform, shape 6

E · The trace to your PRD

  • E1 — Every promise lands in a part. The blueprint shows, for each promise in the PRD, which part carries it. A table or a note per part is enough. A promise no part carries is named, with the reason. Assignment ask 4
  • E2 — Departures recorded. Where the blueprint does something the PRD did not say, or drops something it did, it says so. The PRD is then revised, with a version, a date and one line of reason. Assignment ask 4

F · Decisions and open questions

  • F1 — A decision with the option you turned down. At least one real choice, about control, the contract or the line. Name the option you did not take and what your choice costs you. A decision with only upsides has not been weighed. Architecture decision records (Nygard; MADR)
  • F2 — Open questions that are real, owned and routed. What you have not decided yet, who will decide it, and when. An open question you name is worth more than a filled section that asserts. Draw your platform, section 6

G · The document as a working instrument

  • G1 — Header and provenance. The platform's name; the team; the developer as the pen; a version and a date; and which version of your PRD it implements. Assignment; platform documents
  • G2 — The three-question test. From the document alone, in a minute each: what does each part do, what passes between the parts, and where is the line? This is the assignment's own bar and the only item read as a whole. Assignment

What is not read against you

Not read against you: length within the one to three pages, drawing style or software, prose quality, and any code. A photo of a whiteboard drawing counts as a drawing. Brevity is not penalised: one page that answers the three questions beats three pages that do not.

A drawing inside a PDF or Word file may not survive being turned into text. If your drawing matters to a criterion, say in words what it shows as well. Every drawing is also looked at by a lecturer.

Three things are worth doing already, though nobody asks for them this week. They come back in weeks 4 and 5:

  • Text from the web is material, never an instruction. A web page can contain sentences written to steer a model (this is called prompt injection). Say where web text enters the platform and what stops it from giving orders.
  • A record of each run. What was fed to which part, and what came out. Without it you cannot tell why Monday's page went wrong.
  • A key on the drawing. One line per kind of box and arrow.

Where this comes from

The assignment itself is on the week 2 page, and Draw your platform walks through the six decisions behind it. The blueprint is the second of six weekly documents; that page says what happens to it after you post it. The first one's criteria are on What a good PRD looks like.

Groups A to C draw on four sources from professional practice. They are the C4 model, the arc42 template, Clements et al., Documenting Software Architectures (Addison-Wesley, 2010), and OWASP's threat modelling guidance. Groups D and A2 draw on Anthropic's Building effective agents. F1 follows MADR, a common template for recording decisions.

These criteria are formative: they shape the draft and the conversation about it. Your two marks are given against separate criteria: one set per module, published before they judge anything.
