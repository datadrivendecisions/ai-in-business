Prepare your interview

Your field visit is the moment your research design meets the company you are writing the handbook for. You get one conversation. This page is how you earn it.

Homework for week 3, one guide per team. You turn your research proposal and your PRD into a guide for that one conversation. You also make sure the company gets something out of it.

You build the guide in three rounds: your own thinking, then AI, then your partner team. Each round has something from the round before to react to. Keep the guide itself to one or two pages. The logs from rounds 2 and 3 may run longer, because they are your evidence and not something you hand the company.

Round 1 — your own thinking, no AI yet

Sit down as a team and draft this from your research proposal and your PRD, before anyone opens an AI tool. Cover four things.

1. What the visit is for, in one paragraph the company can read

Not your research question — their stake in it. Why is twenty minutes of their time worth spending, for them? This paragraph is what you send ahead, or what you open with.

2. Questions, traced to your proposal

For each question, name the sub-question from your reworked research proposal that it answers. Then say why this needs their answer rather than a desk source. A question you could answer by reading a report is a desk-research question. Cut it.

Group your questions. The internal-analysis ones — their situation, their capabilities, what they have actually tried — stay clearly separate from anything that checks your external analysis against their experience.

3. What you give back

Interviews that only take are the ones companies stop returning calls for. Decide before you go on one concrete thing this SME gets from the conversation. A short write-up of what you learn that is useful to them specifically. A quick reflection on where you see them on the AI maturity ladder. An offer to share the finished handbook page once it has been checked.

Say what it is and when they get it. This is what makes the guide worth both sides' time.

4. How you will run it

Who asks, who takes notes, how you will record it if at all. Then the consent and confidentiality points you cover before a single question — the ones already agreed for this module. Nobody approaches a company before the consent text is signed off, and nothing identifying goes further than agreed. Write it here in your own words, as you will say it in the room.

Do not show this draft to AI before it exists

The point of round 1 is a guide that is genuinely yours, with your own blind spots still in it. Round 2 needs something honest to react to, and round 3 needs something to catch that AI did not already smooth over.

Round 2 — AI makes the questions better

Take the round 1 draft and pressure-test it. A roleplay only improves the guide if it produces evidence you act on. It is easy to finish one feeling reassured, and change nothing.

  1. Brief the AI as your SME, not as a generic owner. Use the description from your research proposal: sector, size, role, what you already know about their relationship to AI. A vague persona produces a vague conversation, and tells you nothing about this company.
  2. Run the whole guide in one sitting, in order, without editing between questions. Fixing a question the moment it wobbles tells you nothing about how it would land in the real room.
  3. Read the transcript back for specific tells. Not a general impression — the list below.
  4. Then flip roles. Ask the same AI, now as an outside reviewer, which of your questions it found hardest to answer honestly, and why. Compare that to what actually happened in the transcript. The two will not always agree, and where they differ is worth a second look.
  5. Log it as a table: question → what happened → what you changed → why. That table is your evidence for how AI helped. A claim that you used it is not.
  6. Say what this cannot tell you. An AI playing your SME is not your SME. It will not know their actual week, and it will not stay politely vague the way a real person sometimes does. Every improvement here is a hypothesis that the real interview tests.

The tells, and what each one means

What you see in the transcriptWhat it tells you about the question
A vague or generic answerThe question is too abstract.
“I’d have to check”You are asking for something they do not have to hand.
“What do you mean by…?”Jargon or ambiguity on your side.
One word, where you wanted a storyThe question is closed and should be open.
An answer you could have found in a desk sourceIt is redundant. Cut it.
A guarded or defensive toneThe question reads like an audit. Say the “what you give back” part earlier, out loud.

If you have time, run it twice with different persona traits — sceptical about AI, then enthusiastic about it. A question that only survives the friendly version is worth a second look.

Round 3 — exchange with your partner team

Send your round 2 guide to the same partner team you have had since week 2. They read it as if they were about to run this interview themselves, and note three things.

  • What would we ask that is not here? The gap that two rounds of your own thinking, plus AI, still did not surface.
  • What confused us, reading it cold? Anything that needed your team's shared context to make sense will need it in the room too.
  • What would this company probably raise that the guide does not ask about? Their outside view of your target SME, not yours.

Revise once more. In your log, mark which suggestions changed the guide and which ones you deliberately did not take, and why. Your portfolio already asks for this record at the module's other gates. What counts is not that you were questioned, but what you did about it.

Hand-in

What
One interview guide per team, with both logs attached — round 2 and round 3.
Who
The team's, not yours alone. What you changed because of this belongs in your own portfolio entry.
Where and when
By Friday morning, 25 September. It goes in your team's Teams channel: the file itself, or a link to a single page on GitHub. A whole repository or a Google Doc cannot be read, and counts as not handed in — the same rule as the week 2 blueprint. Send it as soon as it is ready; do not wait for the deadline.
What comes back
A green light in the same channel, before your team schedules the interview. You need the green light and signed consent. Neither one replaces the other.

This is not scored, and it is not one of the module's two standing gates. It is a readiness check, the same category as the consent sign-off that is already required before any team may approach a company.

The bar

  • A company reading the guide recognises a conversation worth having, and not a form to fill in.
  • Every question traces to a sub-question in your proposal.
  • The logs show the guide actually changed shape twice — once because of AI, once because of your partner team. Changes in wording do not count.

Your field visit is planned across weeks 2 to 4, so it should be booked by now. Week 3 has the rest of this week's work, and Course tools has the other instruments.
