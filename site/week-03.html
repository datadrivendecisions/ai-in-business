Week 3 — can you trust it?

For two weeks you have asked what AI can do for an SME. This week the owner asks something harder: can I trust it with my data, with its answers and with my people? And can I trust the team that tells me so?

Everything you need for the third session is on this page: what to bring, what happens in the room, and the assignments in full. AIBS asks for one handbook page; AEL asks for one document.

Before you come

When and where
Monday 21 September 2026. Room and start time are in your timetable, as in weeks 1 and 2. This is the third of six Mondays. Be on time this week. The session starts with an exercise, and it cannot be joined halfway.
Bring
A charged laptop, and the AI assistant you normally use, signed in. Any tool, any vendor. You need both in the first hour, on your own, so check on Sunday that you can log in.
Also bring
Your team's week 2 hand-in: the handbook page, and the write-up of your search strategy and quality criteria. In the session you look back at both. Did you apply the criteria you wrote down?
Read
Nothing new this week. That is deliberate.
Do
Hand in week 2's work before the session starts, and write your second portfolio entry.

Week 3's theme is trust and AI safety. When can a regional SME trust what an AI tool tells it, and what the tool does on its behalf? What should never leave the building? And what happens inside a firm when people use AI and do not say so? It follows from week 2. There you asked who owns the tools. This week you ask what an owner can safely hand to them.

What happens in the session

One room, both lecturers, one break. The session has three jobs: an exercise you take part in, the research methods of the week's theme, and this week's AEL document. The slides are published after the session.

The first two and a half hours are fixed. The times after that are approximate and are confirmed in the session.

  • 0:00–1:00

    A class exercise — first on your own, then with your team

    You work alone on your laptop, in silence, with your own AI assistant at hand. The lecturer puts the address on the screen and explains how to take part. It has no grade consequence, and you can stop at any point. Then your team meets for the first time that day, to write one short sheet together.

  • 1:00–1:10

    Break

    Before you go: set AI to work on something. Rule 1 of the rulebook.

  • 1:10–2:30

    The exercise, explained

    What it measured. What the room's own numbers show, and what they are too few to show. Then you check the judgement those numbers rest on. You can withdraw your data afterwards, and the lecturer explains how.

  • 2:30–3:00

    AIBS — research methods when AI is part of the process

    Desk research now includes a tool you design yourself. Field research brings data nobody else has. Both are held to the same three questions: is it reliable, is it valid, is it transparent? Then your week 2 page. How did your search strategy work out, did you keep to your quality criteria, and how good is the result? And trust, three ways: can you trust the tool, can the reader trust you, and whose work is it?

  • 3:00–3:15

    AI agents — what you have been using since week 1

    The command-line tool from the week 1 spike gets a name. Then the part of an agent a firm builds and owns, and three questions an SME asks before it trusts one.

  • 3:15–3:35

    AEL — the knowledge architecture

    The third of six documents. It covers where your platform keeps its sources and drafts, and how it finds them again when it needs them. It is the choice the rest of your platform has to live with. What the document must contain is in the assignment below.

  • 3:35–3:55

    Team work time

    The page and the knowledge architecture both start here. Both lecturers circulate: AIBS on the sources and the question your owner asks, AEL on the architecture. Expect to be asked where an answer on your page came from, and whether your platform could tell you.

  • 3:55–4:00

    The rulebook, and close

    Rules 1 and 2 stand. Rule 3 is the one the room writes today. Then what goes in your third portfolio entry.

The assignments

The weekly shape holds: one published handbook page per team on the week's theme, and one AEL document posted in your team's channel.

AIBS — the handbook page: trust and AI safety

One page of your team's handbook, written for the SME you named in your research proposal. It answers the question that owner would ask: can I trust this — with my data, with its answers, and with my people? Take the three parts in turn. What leaves the building when the firm uses AI, and could it run the model itself? When may an AI tool act on the firm's behalf, and when only advise? And what happens when staff use AI and do not say so?

It follows the chapter template and meets the four criteria on it. Every claim carries an appraised source: who says it, and one line on why they are worth believing. Say on the page what your platform drafted and what your team checked. A reader who cannot tell will trust the whole page less. It ends in something the owner can do.

Scope and depth are confirmed in the session.

The bar: an owner reading it knows what to hand to AI, what to keep in the building, and what to ask their staff.

AEL — the knowledge architecture

The third of the six documents. It extends your blueprint: one to three pages of boxes and words, with no code. The whole team reads it, because every later week builds on this choice.

  1. What the platform has to know. The sources, the drafts, the context file from week 2, and anything else it reads. Say where each one comes from.
  2. Where it lives, and how it is found again. The platform can look up passages in the documents at the moment a question is asked. Or it can compile the sources into pages of its own ahead of time. Or you can choose another way. Name your choice, and trace the path from a question to the passage or page that answers it.
  3. Why this, and not the alternatives. Weigh at least one alternative. The choice follows from what your platform's job is, so point to the PRD promise it serves. Name what would make you switch.
  4. The line, kept. Your blueprint names a line nothing may cross. Show where material on the protected side is stored, and what stops the platform from reading it into a model service.
  5. The open questions. The Socratic tutor sent your team questions about the blueprint. Give each one a short answer: what you changed, or why you left it.

The decision log starts this week. From now on your team keeps one entry per choice: what failed, and what you changed because of it. Its first entry is this architecture. The log travels with the first evaluation in week 5. See the six documents.

The criteria the knowledge architecture is read against are published here before the deadline, as the blueprint's were.

Done when a reader can tell: where your sources and drafts live, how the platform finds them, and why you chose that way.

How they fit together, this week

The page asks whether an owner can trust an answer. The knowledge architecture asks the same thing of your own platform. When it writes a sentence on your page, can you show which source it came from? A platform that cannot show you where an answer came from is the kind of tool your page tells owners to be careful with.

Hand-in

What
One published handbook page per team, the knowledge architecture with the first entry of your decision log, and one portfolio entry per person.
Who
The page, the architecture and the log are the team's. The portfolio is yours alone, and nobody else writes in it.
Where and when
Due by the start of the next session — Monday 28 September. The page is handed in by being published on the shared site. The knowledge architecture goes in your team's Teams channel: the file itself, or a link to a single page on GitHub. A whole repository or a Google Doc cannot be read, and counts as not handed in.
The gates
Your page goes to your partner team. Your knowledge architecture goes to the Socratic tutor. It reads it beside your PRD and your blueprint, and sends back three to five questions in the same channel. Neither gate is scored. What you changed because of them goes in your portfolio, and that is assessable.

The page and the architecture are not marked. They are evidence for your final interview — see how you are assessed. Your portfolio is the part that carries a mark: 30 % of both your marks, and this is the third of six entries.

Your third portfolio entry

The same four standard questions as last week, in about a page:

  • What did your team publish? One or two sentences on the page and what it argues.
  • What did you contribute to it? Named and specific. A small contribution you can back up is worth more than a large one you cannot.
  • What did the gates ask, and what did you change? This week there are two sets of tutor questions to answer: the ones on your blueprint, and the ones on this architecture.
  • What did your build role produce? The architecture, the first log entry, the part you built. Whatever your role put out this week.

After week 3

Field research is planned across weeks 2 to 4, so your visit should be booked by now. The rule has not changed. Nobody approaches a company before the consent and anonymisation text is signed off. Interview material never goes through a free-tier AI tool, and your knowledge architecture now says where it is kept instead.

The week map has week 4 as SME knowledge gaps, with the build plan as the fourth of the six documents. That page appears here about a week before its session.
