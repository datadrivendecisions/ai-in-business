1. What a blueprint is

A blueprint is a drawing plus a few sentences. It says what the parts of your platform are, what passes between them, and where the line runs that nothing may cross. One to three pages. The developer holds the pen and the whole team reads it, because the promises it has to carry are the PRD’s.

It is the second of six documents, and each one needs the one before it.

2. The map

People have been arranging software into parts for fifty years, and the arrangements that keep working have names. Two words are worth separating first.

A style is the overall shape of a system — how it is divided up, at the largest scale. A pattern is a worked solution to one problem inside that shape. Styles are the broad strokes; patterns are the detail.

Below are ten styles. The colour is our verdict for this platform, in these six weeks. Select one to read what it is and what it would cost you.

3. Two altitudes

Here is the mistake to avoid. The same platform has two true pictures, and most first blueprints draw only one.

Say which altitude you are at, on the drawing. Two students arguing about a blueprint are usually standing at different altitudes and have not noticed. It costs you a label and saves you an hour.

4. Six shapes worth stealing

Out of the ten styles, six earn a place. Each answers a question you actually have.

From service-oriented architecture

1. Parts are roles

Give each part a job you could write on a business card: finds sources, appraises them, drafts, checks, publishes. One sentence each. If the sentence needs an “and”, it is two parts.

Resist slicing into twenty tiny parts. A company of twenty agents is not a company, it is a crowd — and you have four people and six weeks to run it.

Orchestration or choreography

2. One boss, or a wire

Either one part decides what happens next and calls the others, or parts react to things that happened — source appraised, draft ready — with no centre.

For six weeks and four people: start with one boss. When a page fails to come out on Monday there is one place to look.

From data-centric architecture

3. The shared desk

Agents have no corridor. They overhear nothing and remember nothing unless you write it down. Put the target SME, the quality criteria and the house style in one place every part reads.

This is the failure nobody sees coming: two parts quietly working from two versions of the house style, and six pages that do not cohere.

Hexagonal, or ports and adapters

4. One door to the outside

Model services, the open web, your partner firm, the published site: all outside. Put the traffic through named doors, and the rule about what may pass becomes something you can point at on the drawing.

It also buys you the manual fallback for free. A door with two ways through it — the automatic one and a person — is the same door.

Microkernel and plug-ins

5. A small core, and hires

Keep a small centre that does not change: the runner, the shared desk, the house rules. Everything you add in weeks 3 to 6 arrives beside it rather than inside it.

This is what stops week 4 being a rewrite of week 2. Adding a capability should feel like hiring.

Pipeline, or pipe and filter

6. A line with files between

Each stage takes a file and produces a file, and that file is the contract. Two of you can then build two stages at once, because you agreed the file before anything else.

And any stage can be done by a person instead, because the handover is a file rather than a function call. Your manual fallback stops being extra work.

5. Where the map stops

Now the honest part. That catalogue was written for ordinary software, where a part given the same input returns the same answer and can be tested. Your parts are not like that. They are workers with judgement, they fail unpredictably, and — the awkward one — they fail plausibly. A wrong answer arrives in the same confident prose as a right one.

Three things a company of agents needs that none of the ten styles names.

Week 4 and 5 · evaluation

Who checks the work

Something has to hold the output against the criteria your PRD set, before it goes out. A part, a person, or both.

Week 5 · monitoring

What happens when it is wrong

Not if. Does the work stop, go back a stage, or reach a person? Silence is also an answer, and it is the one that publishes the mistake.

Week 6 · skills and memory

What is carried, and what is looked up

What does every part know by default, what does it fetch when needed, and what does it forget on purpose?

So the map gets you through weeks 2 and 3 and then stops, precisely where weeks 4, 5 and 6 begin. The parts of your platform that make it a company of agents rather than a program are the parts the tradition has no vocabulary for. That is not a gap in your reading — it is where the subject currently ends, and it is why this module exists.

6. The six decisions

Everything above is preparation for these. Answer them as a team, out loud, in this order. Each one contributes a sentence to a summary at the foot of the page that you can paste into your blueprint and then improve.

Leaving one open is allowed. An open question you name, with the reason it is open, is worth more in this document than a filled section that asserts. Anything you leave blank appears in the summary as an open question, which is exactly where it belongs.

Decision 1 · the parts

What is a part, in your platform?

This decides every other box on the drawing, so settle it first.

Decision 2 · control

Who decides what happens next?

This is your management structure, and it is the decision teams most often leave implicit.

Decision 3 · the contract

What passes between two parts?

Name one seam and its contract. One is enough for this week, and it is what lets two of you build two parts at once.

The seam most teams here will need. Your best sources on a regional manufacturer are in Dutch — the Chamber of Commerce, the sector bodies, the regional press. Your handbook is in English. Somewhere a Dutch source becomes an English claim with its meaning and its attribution intact.

Which part does that, and what does it hand on? Name it, and a fact about your team stops being a problem about who is in it. It becomes the clearest thing your platform is for.

Decision 4 · shared memory

Where does the shared context live?

Your target SME, your quality criteria, what is out of scope, your house style. Every part needs them and no part can overhear them.

Decision 5 · the line

Who is allowed to talk to the outside world?

Every blueprint this week must carry one sentence about a line that nothing crosses. This decides where that sentence can be enforced.

Decision 6 · being wrong

What happens when a part is confidently wrong?

This is the one the architecture books do not answer for you. It will happen in week 3, so decide it in week 2.

Six sentences to paste and improve

This is a skeleton and it is nobody’s finished document. Paste it into your blueprint, put your own words over it, and add the drawing — which is the part that actually does the work.

Then hand it in. One to three pages, in your team’s channel, by the start of the next session. The file itself, or a link to a single page. It goes to the Socratic tutor, which reads it beside your PRD and asks whether they still describe one platform. Three to five questions come back. No mark ever does.

The criteria your blueprint is read against are published on this site before the deadline, as the PRD’s were. Hold your draft against them yourself before anyone else does.

Words on this page

Part

One piece of your platform with one job, which you can describe in a single sentence. Drawn as a box. It might be an agent, a script, or a person following a checklist.

Contract

What two parts have agreed to hand over: a named file with named fields, or a message with a known shape. The agreement is the contract, not the code that keeps it.

Seam

The join between two parts — the place a contract sits. Naming one seam properly is worth more than naming six vaguely.

Orchestrator

A part whose job is deciding what happens next and calling the other parts in order. A managing editor.

Port and adapter

A door in the edge of your system (the port), and the particular thing plugged into it today (the adapter). Swapping a model vendor should mean changing the adapter and nothing else.

Manual fallback

A way for a person to do, by hand, what an automatic part normally does — without the rest of the platform noticing the difference. A course rule, and a design property you get almost free if your contracts are files.

Style and pattern

A style is the overall shape of a system. A pattern is a worked solution to one problem inside that shape. Layered is a style; clean architecture is a pattern within it.
